## 1. What problem does ATTACKDNA address, and what is its core idea?

### Takeaway
ATTACKDNA starts from the problem that "every organisation solves the same attack twice". The useful part of an incident report is buried in data that cannot be shared. The project's answer is to separate the attack from the victim. It removes the victim's identity from the report and keeps the attack's behaviour as a structured, searchable fingerprint (the "Attack DNA"). With the security team's approval, that fingerprint goes into a shared memory. A later organisation hit by the same pattern recalls it, sees why it matched and what worked, and never learns who was attacked.

### Cited Findings
- Tagline, quoted exactly: "One organisation's cyber incident becomes another organisation's defensive knowledge, without sharing the victim's identity or its report." ([README.md L3-4](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))
- Problem statement, quoted exactly: "Every organisation solves the same attack twice. The first time it is an incident; the second time it should be a lookup. It usually isn't, because the useful part of an incident report is buried inside data nobody is allowed to share." ([README.md L6-8](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))
- Core idea, quoted exactly: "ATTACKDNA separates the two. It strips the victim's identity out of an incident report and keeps the attack's *behaviour*, its **Attack DNA**, as a searchable record. With the security team's approval, that fingerprint joins a **shared memory**: the next organisation hit by the same pattern recalls it, sees why it matched and what worked, and never learns who was attacked." ([README.md L10-14](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))
- Who uses it: "the security team of a member organisation, holding an incident report about an attack on their own organisation. The interface is a working tool for that team, not a guided demo" ([frontend/app.py L1-6](/home/user/attackdna/frontend/app.py)).
- The Attack DNA is defined as "the reusable, victim-free fingerprint of an incident: what kind of attack it was, which ATT&CK behaviours it used, which known-exploited vulnerabilities were involved, what classes of indicator appeared, and what the business impact was. Two incidents at two different organisations that share a DNA signature are comparable — which is what makes a past incident's response reusable today." ([backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py L1-9](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py))
- The repository's own self-description: "ATTACKDNA is a working prototype: it shows the whole idea end to end, offline, on one machine, and measures itself on data it was not tuned on. It is not yet a product." ([README.md L122-124](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))
- The sector is excluded on purpose, as a privacy decision: "Attack DNA does not record which industry the victim is in. Once names are removed, the sector is itself a clue to who the victim was, so it is neither inferred nor shared, and matching uses behaviour alone." ([README.md L87-91](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)). This was introduced in commit `c65101a` (2026-11-13, "Stop inferring or sharing the victim's sector").
- The README's reference list puts the work next to MITRE ATT&CK (Strom et al. 2018), the CISA KEV catalog, MISP (Wagner et al., ACM WISCS 2016), STIX 2.1 (OASIS 2021), Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych 2019), MiniLM (Wang et al. 2020), RAG (Lewis et al. 2020) and TF-IDF term weighting (Salton and Buckley 1988) ([README.md L596-608](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).
- Team listed in the README: Aryam Al-Qahtani (@aryam-Q) and Norah Almishdaq (@norahs21) ([README.md L612-615](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).

### Inferences
- The difference from classic threat-intelligence sharing in the README's own terms: ATTACKDNA shares a behaviour fingerprint plus the response that worked. MISP and STIX, which the README cites, share indicators and objects. The README treats victim-linked indicators (IPs, domains, mailboxes) as data to remove, not to share (see section 2, IoC split). This is a useful point of contrast with MISP/STIX-style sharing in the related-work section.

### Gaps
- The repository has no written problem analysis with external statistics (for example, how often attacks repeat across organisations). The motivation is stated, not quantified.

## 2. Architecture, stage by stage, and data sources with counts

### Takeaway
There is one pipeline function, `analyze()`. It runs: (1) a deterministic privacy layer; (2) Attack DNA extraction, using rules first, an optional LLM merged conservatively, and a hybrid technique mapper with a trained logistic-regression classifier over MiniLM vectors of MITRE's ~17.6k procedure sentences; (3) vector memory (MiniLM through ChromaDB, with a TF-IDF fallback) followed by an explainable structural re-rank; (4) mitigation memory, which keeps recalled, framework and baseline actions apart; (5) a safe tabletop simulation, which is computed but no longer shown in the UI. Sharing is a separate consent step, `contribute()`. It re-checks privacy, sanitizes the team's notes, stores the organisation only as an HMAC pseudonym and stores the share month rather than the incident date. "Ask the memory" (`guidance.py`) reuses the same pipeline on a live description and returns three answers. The knowledge bases are 709 ATT&CK techniques, 1,695 KEV CVEs, 44 mitigations, 172 groups, 825 software, 56 campaigns and 17,632 procedure examples.

### Cited Findings

**Pipeline and entry points**
- Pipeline diagram (README): ① Privacy Layer "deterministic redaction" → ② Attack DNA extraction "hybrid rules + LLM" → knowledge bases "MITRE ATT&CK 709 techniques", "CISA KEV 1,695 KEV CVEs", "Vector Memory ChromaDB", "ATT&CK CTI 44 mitigations 172 groups", "IoC profile shareable vs. victim-linked" → ③ Similar Incidents "explainable re-ranking" → ④ Mitigation Memory "what worked before, cited" → ⑤ Safe Simulation "defensive tabletop exercise" ([README.md L93-116](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).
- There are three entry points. `analyze` is "read-only. Runs the whole chain and returns the result without writing anything". `contribute` is "the security team's consent step. Stores an incident that has already been analysed, from its sanitized text and DNA alone… The raw report is not an input". `ingest` is "analyze + persist in one call, used for loading corpora". "The privacy check is a gate, not a label. If the self-check still finds an identifier, the text is not sent to the optional LLM and it cannot be contributed to shared memory." ([backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py L1-29](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py))
- In code, `analyze()` calls `sanitize()` and `verify_clean()`, sets `use_llm = use_llm and not leaks`, then runs `extract_dna`, `find_similar_incidents`, `recall_mitigations` and optionally `build_simulation` ([analyze.py L48-108](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py)).

**① Privacy layer / sanitizer** (`sanitizer.py`, `name_detector.py`, `lexicon.py`)
- "Redaction is done with rules and word lists, not a model. A rule cannot decide to leak an IP address, and the same report always gives the same output." Identifiers with a shape (addresses, mailboxes, host names, accounts, ticket numbers) are found by regular expressions. Identifiers without a shape (people, organisations, places) are found by reading capitalisation against three vocabularies: "ordinary English words, public security terms (every technique, malware, group, vendor and product name MITRE and CISA publish), and given names. A capitalised token that is in none of them is a name." ([README.md L249-263](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))
- The README lists these properties. "Fails closed" (where it cannot tell a name from a word, it removes it). "Text in a script it cannot read — Arabic has no capital letter to mark a name — is withheld whole". It "Reads the sentence, not just the token". It is "Analytically lossless" (the same value always maps to the same token). It "Preserves the fingerprint" (CVE ids, ATT&CK ids, file hashes and registry keys are protected). It is "Self-checking": "`verify_clean()` … is a self-check, not independent verification — the independent measurement is the hand-marked audit" ([README.md L265-280](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).
- The redaction pattern labels in code are SECRET, EMAIL, URL, IP, MAC, IBAN, CREDIT_CARD, NATIONAL_ID, PHONE, USER_PATH, ACCOUNT, REFERENCE and HOSTNAME ([sanitizer.py L96+](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/sanitizer.py)). PHASE_COVERAGE says "`REDACTION_PATTERNS` — 15 entity types" ([docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md](/home/user/attackdna/docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md)). Text in an unreadable script gets the label `ARABIC_TEXT` or `FOREIGN_TEXT` ([sanitizer.py L398](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/sanitizer.py)).
- What the layer does not claim: "that free text is anonymous. A date or a size can still narrow down a victim; for that reason the industry is not recorded at all." ([README.md L290-292](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))

**② Attack DNA extraction** (`dna_extractor.py`, `mitre_mapper.py`, `example_mapper.py`, `negation.py`, `kev_enricher.py`, `ioc_extractor.py`, `attribution.py`)
- DNA fields: `attack_type`, `initial_vector`, `techniques`/`tactics` "each with the evidence behind it", `cve_details`/`kev`, `impacts`, `severity` ("Derived from impact, KEV status and chain length"), `signature` ("A compact, kill-chain-ordered fingerprint") ([README.md L296-306](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)). Attack type and entry vector are kept apart on purpose: "phishing that ends in ransomware is a **ransomware** incident that *arrived by* phishing" ([README.md L308-310](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).
- Attack-type taxonomy in code (the keys of `ATTACK_TYPES`): ransomware, phishing, business_email_compromise, data_breach, web_exploitation, credential_attack, insider_threat, supply_chain, denial_of_service, malware_infection, plus "unknown" ([dna_extractor.py L34-130](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)). PHASE_COVERAGE says "10 types + decisive markers" and "6 impact classes" ([docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md](/home/user/attackdna/docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md)). The impact classes in code are data_encrypted, data_exfiltrated, service_disruption, financial_loss, credential_compromise and regulatory_exposure ([dna_extractor.py L241-262](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)).
- Rules and the optional LLM: "a deterministic rule layer always runs and always produces a complete DNA; an optional LLM layer enriches it (narrative summary, extra techniques, extra impacts) and is merged conservatively" ([dna_extractor.py L11-14](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)). In `_merge_llm`, "The rules win on ties". "The LLM only decides classification where the rules found nothing". Techniques the LLM adds are re-run through the mapper. The summary and root cause are taken from the LLM. The mode becomes "hybrid (rules + LLM)", otherwise "rule-based" ([dna_extractor.py L428-458](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)).
- LLM providers: the default models are Anthropic `claude-opus-5` and Gemini `gemini-2.5-flash` ([anthropic_provider.py L18](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/llm_providers/anthropic_provider.py), [gemini_provider.py L37](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/llm_providers/gemini_provider.py)). The provider is inferred from the key prefix (`AIza...` or `sk-ant-...`). "No API key is required. Without one the system runs in **rule-based mode**" ([README.md L214-232](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)). The UI uses the LLM automatically whenever a key is available (`use_llm = llm_client.is_available()`, [frontend/app.py L330](/home/user/attackdna/frontend/app.py)).
- Technique mapping has three routes: "an id the report states, a phrase from a curated table, or **resemblance to the ~17,600 sentences MITRE has labelled** (`example_mapper.py`)". It "abstains unless its neighbours agree, and attaches the MITRE sentence as evidence rather than the report's own words. Nothing the report denies is counted: 'no data was exfiltrated' is not exfiltration (`negation.py`)." ([README.md L312-318](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))
- Hybrid mapper and classifier constants: `CLASSIFIER_WEIGHT = 0.7` ("70% classifier, 30% nearest-example votes"), `PICK_FLOOR = 0.2`, `MEANING_GATE = 0.5`, `REPORT_FLOOR = 0.7`, `MAX_LEARNED_PER_REPORT = 5`, `NEIGHBOURS = 10`, `VOTE_FLOOR = 2.0`, `SIMILARITY_FLOOR = 0.35`. These were "Chosen on development data only: a split carved from the training sources for single sentences, and 76 team-labelled development reports for whole reports." ([example_mapper.py L55-83](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/example_mapper.py)). "The classifier is a small logistic regression over MiniLM vectors, trained once by `scripts/train_technique_classifier.py` and committed as `data/processed/technique_classifier.npz` (about 1 MB); nothing is fine-tuned." ([README.md L566-573](/home/user/attackdna/README.md))
- How the mapper evolved, all on the same held-out MITRE sentences: rules alone recall 20.8%, then with the TF-IDF learned mapper 44.0% (commit `ea7c40c`). Then: "rules + TF-IDF 44.0% recall / 62.2% precision / 51.5% F1; rules + hybrid 64.9% / 61.5% / 63.2%" (commit `412a3b8`, MiniLM embeddings of "the same 17,629 examples"). Then the classifier: "recall 81.6% (was 64.9%), precision 60.1%" (commit `f1c70e0`).
- Schema check: "Every extraction is checked against the DNA schema (`validate_dna`), including the privacy invariant that `embedding_text` may never contain a redaction token or an email address." ([README.md L320-323](/home/user/attackdna/README.md); [dna_extractor.py L581-622](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py))
- IoC split. Shareable indicators are "file hashes, CVEs, ransomware extensions, protocols, ports, registry keys". Victim-linked indicators are "addresses, domains, mailboxes, hosts, accounts. Removed, but their *shape* is kept: '3 distinct network addresses, 1 mailbox'" ([README.md L325-335](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).
- Threat-actor resemblance compares the technique set "against the documented TTPs of 172 ATT&CK threat groups and 825 malware families and tools, weighted by technique specificity". Matches are labelled "`weak`, `moderate` or `notable` — **never 'confirmed'**" ([README.md L337-350](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)). The code thresholds are `MIN_COVERAGE = 0.30`, notable ≥ 0.75 and moderate ≥ 0.50 ([attribution.py L30-37](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/attribution.py)).

**③ Vector memory** (`vector_memory.py`)
- "Only DNA-derived text is embedded — never the report — so the vector store is structurally incapable of holding victim identifiers." Backends in order of preference: 1. `sentence-transformers` (`all-MiniLM-L6-v2`); 2. "ChromaDB's bundled ONNX MiniLM — the same model, no torch (**default**)"; 3. "TF-IDF — no downloads, works fully offline". ChromaDB is installed from a separate requirements file because `onnxruntime` "fails to resolve on 3.13" ([README.md L352-365](/home/user/attackdna/README.md); [vector_memory.py L1-17, L245-260, L350-363](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/vector_memory.py)).
- The embedded text is built only from DNA fields: attack type, initial vector, tactics, technique ids and names, CVEs, impacts, and `summary` ([dna_extractor.py L522-538](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)).
- Relevance floors depend on the backend. "MiniLM 0.64 - 0.86 [relevant] 0.53 - 0.70 [off-topic] floor 0.73; TF-IDF 0.03 - 0.59 / 0.00 - 0.06 floor 0.10", "Measured on the 24-incident demo corpus" ([vector_memory.py L383-404](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/vector_memory.py)).

**④ Explainable re-ranking** (`similarity.py`)
- Retrieval has two stages. Recall over-fetches `max(top_k * 3, 10)` candidates from vector memory. They are then re-ranked on structural overlap. "Never sector: the victim's industry is not recorded." ([README.md L367-376](/home/user/attackdna/README.md); [similarity.py L108-116](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/similarity.py))
- Weights: `SEMANTIC_WEIGHT = 0.45` and `STRUCTURAL_WEIGHT = 0.55`. Inside the structural score: techniques 0.45 (Jaccard), tactics 0.20 (Jaccard), attack_type 0.18, initial_vector 0.10, cves 0.07. Every match carries `match_reasons` and its semantic and structural sub-scores ([similarity.py L28-41, L136-170](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/similarity.py)). Defaults: `SIMILARITY_TOP_K` = 5 and `SIMILARITY_THRESHOLD` = 0.25 ([config.py L77-79](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/config.py)).
- The README's justification: "on the demo corpus, five ransomware incidents all score 0.92–0.97 semantically, and only structural overlap separates them" ([README.md L387-389](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).

**⑤ Mitigation memory** (`mitigation_memory.py`)
- Recommendations have three origins, each labelled: `recalled` ("What **this organisation** actually did before, cited back to the incident"), `framework` ("An official MITRE ATT&CK mitigation (M####)"), and `suggested` ("A concrete baseline control filling a gap neither covers") ([README.md L391-403](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).
- Ranking in code: each recalled action scores the sum of similarity × effectiveness over the incidents that used it (0.6 when no effectiveness was recorded), multiplied by `(1 + 0.15 × (times_used − 1))`. At most 12 actions are kept, sorted by response phase and then by score ([mitigation_memory.py L99-160](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/mitigation_memory.py)). The UI shows "The three strongest actions in each phase" (`ACTIONS_PER_PHASE = 3`, [frontend/app.py L104](/home/user/attackdna/frontend/app.py)).

**⑥ Safe simulation** (`simulator.py`)
- A "**defensive tabletop exercise**: injects the SOC is told about, the detection that should fire, its telemetry source, the decision the team must make, and the response actions… It contains no payloads, commands, exploit code, phishing copy or attacker infrastructure — and `tests/test_pipeline.py` asserts that." ([README.md L417-427](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)). It is no longer shown on screen (see section 5).

**Cross-organisation contribution with keyed pseudonyms** (`contributors.py`, `contribute()`)
- The organisation's identity is "HMAC-SHA256 of the normalised organisation name under a server-side key". The key comes from `ATTACKDNA_PSEUDONYM_KEY`, or else from `backend/.pseudonym_key`, which is generated automatically. "The pseudonym never leaves the server. API responses and the UI only carry the result of the comparison (`from_your_organisation`), so even a reader of every response cannot tell whether two shared incidents came from the same contributor." The comparison is constant-time (`hmac.compare_digest`) ([contributors.py L1-76](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/contributors.py)).
- What `contribute()` does: it re-runs `verify_clean()` and raises `PrivacyGateError` when anything remains. It sanitizes the free-text `action` and `notes` of each mitigation. It skips duplicates, defined as the same contributor, the same embedding text and the same sanitized text. It stores `provenance="shared"`, `occurred_at=None`, `raw_text=None`, and `shared_at`/`created_at` set to the first day of the current month. The title is derived only from the DNA ("Ransomware via phishing"). It returns `withheld: ["original report", "organisation name", "removed identifiers", "date of the incident"]` ([analyze.py L146-232](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py)).
- Privacy by construction: `raw_text` is "`NULL` by default". "The token→original map stays in memory for the audit panel; it is never persisted, embedded, or sent to a model". "The Attack DNA itself is assembled from closed vocabularies: enumerated types, ATT&CK ids and names, and MITRE's own example sentences. No free text from the report can enter it" ([README.md L449-474](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).

**"Ask the memory" guidance** (`backend/app/services/guidance.py`)
- "The team describes what has happened so far. That description goes through the same pipeline as a report (privacy layer, Attack DNA, similar incidents, mitigation memory), and this module turns the result into three answers": `what_worked` ("response actions that worked in similar past incidents"), `same_entry` ("past attackers who got in the same way, and what they did") and `next_steps` ("what attackers did next in those incidents, after the furthest point this attack has reached so far"). "`next_steps` is a record of what happened in similar past incidents, not a prediction: it says how many of them went on to each later stage, and nothing more." "Pure functions over plain dicts: no database, no model" ([guidance.py L1-18](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/guidance.py)).
- Mechanics. `what_worked` returns recalled actions first and falls back to baseline (`suggested`) actions only when memory has none (limit 5). `same_entry` filters the similar incidents on an identical `initial_vector` (limit 3) and shows each one's kill chain, up to 6 technique names and its 3 most effective actions. `next_steps` finds the furthest tactic reached, then counts later tactics across the similar incidents, keeping the top 4 with up to 3 example techniques ([guidance.py L41-114](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/guidance.py)).
- In the UI, the description is sanitized first. If the privacy check fails, the page stops. If memory has nothing close it says so. Section 3's caption reads "A record of what happened in similar past incidents, not a prediction." ([frontend/app.py L395-445](/home/user/attackdna/frontend/app.py))
- A second, older Ask-the-memory module exists. `rag_qa.py` does "retrieval-augmented question answering over the corpus", with verified citations and an offline digest. It is exposed at the API route `POST /ask`, but the current UI uses `guidance.py` ([rag_qa.py L1-27](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/rag_qa.py); [routes/search.py L42-56](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/routes/search.py)).

### Inferences
- **The "closed vocabularies" claim holds in rule-based mode but not fully with the LLM on.** `embedding_text` ends with `dna.get('summary', '')`. When the LLM is enabled, that summary is free text the LLM wrote from the sanitized report ([dna_extractor.py L455, L522-538](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)). The README's claim that "No free text from the report can enter it" is therefore strictly true only in rule-based mode, where `_fallback_summary` is a template. The text the LLM sees has already passed the privacy check, so the remaining risk is limited to what an LLM restates from sanitized text.
- **Shared records hold more than the DNA.** They store the sanitized text plus the DNA ([analyze.py L252](/home/user/attackdna/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py)), and the README says the same (L454-455). A privacy miss in the sanitized text therefore sits in shared storage, even though B's screen shows only DNA-derived fields. The cross-organisation run measured this case (one stored record still naming A; see section 3).
- **"Recalled" wording.** The README's line that recalled actions are what "this organisation" did dates from a single-organisation framing. In the cross-organisation flow, recalled actions come from any contributor, and the UI labels them "shared by another organisation" ([frontend/app.py L307, L583-587](/home/user/attackdna/frontend/app.py)).

## 4. Next-step prediction (pull request #16): what it is and what it measured

### Takeaway
A predictive "what the attacker will likely do next" module (`services/next_step.py`) exists only on branch `claude/eager-cray-db8wv0` (commit `6411856`). It is not in `main`. On 177 held-out ATT&CK profiles, shown the first half of the profile, at least one of its 3 predictions was a later step in 56.5% of cases, against 53.1% for always guessing the most common steps. For threat groups and campaigns the figures are 66.7% against 59.0%. The team judged the lead "small and the sample modest" and kept it out of the prototype. In `main`, Ask the memory shows only a record of what similar past incidents did next.

### Cited Findings
- `main` README, quoted exactly: "Predicting the attacker's next step. A first version is kept in pull request #16 (branch `claude/eager-cray-db8wv0`, `services/next_step.py`): shown the first half of 177 held-out ATT&CK profiles, at least one of its three predictions was a step the attacker really took later in 56.5% of cases, against 53.1% for guessing the most common steps (66.7% against 59.0% on the 39 threat groups and campaigns). The lead is small and the sample modest, so it is not part of this prototype; until then, Ask the memory shows what attackers did next in similar past incidents, as a record." ([README.md L148-155](/home/user/attackdna/README.md); added in commit `9e10532`, 2026-11-14)
- `git merge-base --is-ancestor 6411856 HEAD` returns false, so the commit is not in `main`/HEAD.
- Results file on the branch (`git show 6411856:data/evaluation/next_step_results.json`): `held_out_profiles: 186`.
  - "attackers" (threat groups and campaigns): cases 39, found 26, hit_at_3 0.6667, precision_at_3 0.4188, shortcut_found 23, shortcut_hit_at_3 0.5897.
  - "tools" (malware and tools): cases 138, found 74, hit_at_3 0.5362, precision_at_3 0.2705, shortcut_found 71, shortcut_hit_at_3 0.5145.
  - "all": cases 177, found 100, hit_at_3 0.5650, precision_at_3 0.3032, shortcut_found 94, shortcut_hit_at_3 0.5311.
  - `universal_techniques_excluded`: T1027, T1036, T1057, T1059, T1070, T1071, T1082, T1083, T1105, T1140.
- Method (`git show 6411856:backend/app/services/next_step.py`). The pools of "known attacks" are "MITRE ATT&CK's profiles of ~930 threat groups, malware families, tools and campaigns" plus "the incidents in shared memory". The steps: find the furthest kill-chain stage; take the 50 most similar known attacks, weighting each shared technique by its distinctiveness; score later-stage techniques by their weighted share; leave out the ten near-universal techniques. Constants: `NEIGHBOURS = 50`, `PREDICTIONS = 3`, `UNIVERSAL = 10`, `MIN_TECHNIQUES = 4`. "Settings… were chosen on a development split of the ATT&CK profiles".
- Evaluation design (`scripts/evaluate_next_step.py` on the branch): one fifth of the profiles is held out (`HELD_OUT_FRACTION = 5`, `SEED = 7`). Each held-out profile is put in kill-chain order and the system is shown the first half. A prediction counts as correct "when at least one is a technique the attacker really used at a later stage". The baseline is to "always predict the most common later techniques".
- Caveats in the branch README (diff of `6411856`), quoted exactly: "The lead over guessing is clear for threat groups and campaigns and small for malware and tools, whose profiles list what a tool can do rather than the steps of one attack. These profiles summarise many intrusions and their order comes from the kill chain, not from timestamps; 39 attacker profiles is a small sample."
- On the branch, the README headline said "It also looks ahead… shows **what the attacker will likely do next**, with the control that stops each step". The `main` README dropped this and moved the module to future work (commits `9e10532` and `5593867`).

### Inferences
- The evaluation's "next step" is a later kill-chain stage of a technique set that summarises many intrusions. It is not a time-ordered step in one incident, so it is a proxy for real next-step prediction. Against the frequency baseline, the gain is +3.4 points overall (56.5 against 53.1) and +7.7 points for the 39 attacker profiles (66.7 against 59.0). No confidence intervals are given. With n = 39 and n = 177, the intervals would very likely overlap the baseline. That is my inference, not a figure in the repository.

### Gaps
- I did not check the open, closed or merged state of PR #16 on GitHub (no API calls were made, by constraint). The repository only says the module "is kept in pull request #16".

## 5. Implemented versus not yet, and how the team's description compares with the code

### Takeaway

## 6. Stated limitations and future work, and where the documents disagree

### Takeaway
The repository is unusually explicit about its limits:
- small samples, with Wilson intervals given;
- synthetic, team-labelled cross-organisation data;
- no SOC reports and no independent annotation;
- English only, one machine and no accounts;
- removing identifiers is not anonymity.

Planned work: larger independent evaluation sets, an SOC pilot, next-step prediction, STIX 2.1 export, Arabic reports, multi-organisation deployment, and, from PHASE_COVERAGE, federated memory, Sigma output and automatic effectiveness feedback. The two docs under `docs/` still carry older figures and outdated pipeline details, so the newest README and the result JSONs should be treated as authoritative.

### Cited Findings
- README limits and next steps are quoted in section 5 ([README.md L130-158](/home/user/attackdna/README.md)).
- Privacy limit: "Removing identifiers is not full anonymity: details such as timing and scale can still point to an organization." ([crossorg_results.json caveats](/home/user/attackdna/data/evaluation/crossorg_results.json)) And: "What it does not claim: that free text is anonymous." ### Inferences
- For the report, the authoritative current figures are `README.md` at HEAD together with `data/evaluation/results.json`, `mapper_results.json` and `crossorg_results.json`. The 2026-09-08 lines in `docs/` reflect the 8-case development era and should not be quoted as results.
- The strongest honest claims, the ones that survive the caveats:
  - On 20 synthetic A/B pairs, A's incident is in B's top five 19/20 times, with 0/20 screens naming A.
  - The privacy layer cut stored records naming A from 20/20 to 1/20 at no recall cost.
  - Behaviour survives sanitization: attack type 40/40, initial vector 40/40, techniques 72/73.
- The weakest claims: precision@1 (55%, CI 34–74%), next-step prediction (small lead, n = 39 for attackers), and any claim about real SOC reports (none were tested).

### Gaps
- No user study, SOC pilot or practitioner feedback exists in the repository.
- No threat model of the deployed multi-organisation setting exists, for example who holds the pseudonym key or how members are authenticated, because there is no deployment.
