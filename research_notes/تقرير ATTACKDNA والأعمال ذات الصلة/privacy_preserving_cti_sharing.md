# Privacy-Preserving CTI and Incident Sharing Between Organisations: Related Work for ATTACKDNA

How these notes were checked (for the report writer). Bibliographic data comes from Crossref and OpenAlex metadata, or from the publisher or official landing page linked on each item. Abstract-level claims come from the paper's own abstract (OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, arXiv, USENIX, NDSS or AISeL pages). Anything I could not check is marked **[unverified]** or listed under Gaps. Each item ends with an "ATTACKDNA:" line saying how the project differs from it or what it borrows. ATTACKDNA in brief: a deterministic sanitizer strips victim identifiers; the shared memory stores only the "Attack DNA" (attack type, initial vector, ATT&CK techniques, severity, CVEs); contributors appear under keyed pseudonyms.

## Q1. Platforms and standards (MISP, OpenCTI, STIX 2.1/TAXII 2.1, ISACs/ISAOs, CISA AIS, NIST SP 800-150): what do they share, and what are their known limitations?

### Takeaway
The main platforms and standards are built mostly to exchange indicators (IoCs) and STIX objects among trusted, identified members. Privacy comes from access control (distribution levels, sharing groups, TLP-style markings, legal protections), not from removing the victim's identity from the content. Measurement studies find that indicator feeds overlap very little and have limited coverage and timeliness. The US flagship government programme (CISA AIS) lost most of its sharing volume between 2020 and 2022.

### Cited Findings

**MISP**
- **C. Wagner, A. Dulaunoy, G. Wagener, A. Iklody, "MISP: The Design and Implementation of a Collaborative Threat Intelligence Sharing Platform," Proc. 2016 ACM Workshop on Information Sharing and Collaborative Security (WISCS '16, co-located with ACM CCS), pp. 49–56, 2016. DOI 10.1145/2994539.2994542.** MISP is presented as "a trusted platform that allows the collection and sharing of important indicators of compromise (IoC) of targeted attacks," plus vulnerabilities and financial-fraud indicators — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2994539.2994542)
  - ATTACKDNA: MISP's unit of sharing is the IoC event inside a trust group, and the contributing organisation is normally visible. ATTACKDNA shares behaviour-level records whose contributor is pseudonymised by default.
- MISP's current feature page lists "granular sharing controls": distribution levels and custom sharing groups, "down to individual attributes". It also lists "delegated publishing … through a simple, pseudo-anonymous sharing mechanism", GnuPG/S/MIME signing and encryption, and real-time streaming of events and sightings over ZMQ/Kafka — [MISP features](https://www.misp-project.org/features/)
  - ATTACKDNA: MISP's pseudo-anonymity is opt-in and per event: a trusted community re-publishes on your behalf. ATTACKDNA applies keyed pseudonyms to every contribution. It can still cite delegated publishing as precedent that practitioners want attribution-free submission.

**OpenCTI**
- The OpenCTI documentation is organised around knowledge exploration, graphs, an indicators lifecycle, "Data segregation", "Authorized members", "Decay rules" and "Sharing and exports" — [OpenCTI docs](https://docs.opencti.io/latest/)
  - ATTACKDNA: OpenCTI is a knowledge-graph platform that manages a single organisation's or community's CTI with access segregation. It has no built-in victim de-identification of free-text incident reports, so ATTACKDNA's sanitizer covers ground OpenCTI does not. **[My extract did not include an explicit statement of OpenCTI's STIX 2.1 data model; confirm before citing.]**

**STIX 2.1 / TAXII 2.1 (OASIS)**
- **OASIS, "STIX Version 2.1," OASIS Standard, 10 June 2021.** It defines 18 STIX Domain Objects: Attack Pattern, Campaign, Course of Action, Grouping, Identity, Indicator, Infrastructure, Intrusion Set, Location, Malware, Malware Analysis, Note, Observed Data, Opinion, Report, Threat Actor, Tool and Vulnerability. STIX Cyber-observable Objects (SCOs) cover host and network data — [STIX 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html)
- STIX 2.1: "The Incident object in STIX 2.1 is a stub… does not contain properties to represent metadata about incidents"; implementers are told to extend it. `created_by_ref` (the author Identity) is an *optional* common property — [STIX 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html)
  - ATTACKDNA: there is no standard schema for a sanitized incident record, which justifies ATTACKDNA's own "Attack DNA" schema. ATTACKDNA could be exported as a STIX Incident extension that links to Attack Pattern SDOs (ATT&CK IDs) and Vulnerability SDOs (CVEs), with `created_by_ref` pointing to a pseudonymous Identity or left out.
- STIX 2.1: "Attack Patterns are a type of TTP that describe ways that adversaries attempt to compromise targets" (for example, spear phishing) — [STIX 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html)
- **OASIS, "TAXII Version 2.1," OASIS Standard, 10 June 2021** (the transport protocol for STIX) — [TAXII 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/taxii/v2.1/os/taxii-v2.1-os.html)

**CISA Automated Indicator Sharing (AIS)**
- AIS exchanges "machine-readable cyber threat indicators and defensive measures" in real time, using STIX/TAXII 2.1 clients. It costs nothing, but participants need a PKI certificate. The page says only that "CISA has taken careful measures to ensure that participants' privacy and protections are implemented and respected", and that CISA 2015 "grants liability protection, privacy protections, and other protections" — [CISA AIS](https://www.cisa.gov/topics/cyber-threats-and-advisories/information-sharing/automated-indicator-sharing-ais)
- **DHS Office of Inspector General, OIG-24-60 (Sept 2024).** Even after the AIS 2.0 upgrades finished in March 2022, the number of participants sharing through AIS fell to its lowest level since 2017. Sharing of indicators fell 93% from CY2020 to CY2022. CISA paused AIS outreach in May 2022 and kept no expenditure data that would allow AIS costs to be audited — [OIG-24-60](https://oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/2024-09/OIG-24-60-Sep24.pdf); [FedScoop](https://fedscoop.com/cisa-threat-information-sharing-dhs-oig-report/)
- Industry reaction: users said the main problem is "the quality of data they receive back from the system" — [SC World](https://www.scworld.com/news/companies-that-opt-out-of-dhs-threat-sharing-platform-call-for-better-data). SANS objected that the OIG treated AIS as CISA's only real-time sharing mechanism and did not evaluate its impact on prevention — [SANS](https://www.sans.org/blog/reflections-on-the-us-governments-oig-report-on-cisas-automated-indicator-sharing-program)
  - ATTACKDNA: AIS is the clearest real-world evidence that indicator-only sharing with legal shields still loses participants over value and quality. ATTACKDNA's answer is to share higher-value behaviour, and to remove identifying content rather than rely on legal protection.
- **Legal basis (Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015):** the Act's protections lapsed in autumn 2025 and were revived through 30 Jan 2026. They were then extended to 30 Sept 2026 by the 3 Feb 2026 spending law — [Hunton](https://hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/congress-extends-cybersecurity-information-sharing-act-of-2015-through-september-2026); [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/cybersecurity-2/cybersecurity-information-sharing-act-of-2015-reauthorized-through-january-2026/). A Congressional Research Service update dated 3 Sept 2026 says the provisions move to 11 Dec 2026 once the Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027 is enacted — [CRS IF12959](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-03_IF12959_e88b818ccb3aca205dcf1ba233b57cd55207205b.html). If the Act expires, the FOIA exemptions, liability limits and privilege protections for sharing go with it — [CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/cnas-insights/cnas-insights-the-case-for-long-term-cisa-2015-reauthorization)
  - ATTACKDNA: in the US, the willingness to share depends on legal protections that keep expiring. A design that does not reveal *who* was attacked lowers that dependency.

### Inferences
- None of the main platforms (MISP, OpenCTI, AIS) treats "hide the victim inside the content" as a core goal. They protect *who can see* (sharing groups, TLP, PKI, legal shields) but not *what the content reveals*. ATTACKDNA's novelty is to change the shared unit to sanitized behaviour plus a pseudonymous contributor.
- Because the STIX Incident object is only a stub, there is a standards gap that ATTACKDNA's "Attack DNA" record can fill or map onto.

## Q2. Empirical and analytical studies on why organisations do not share (privacy, legal liability, reputation, trust, free-riding)

### Takeaway
Surveys, interview studies and game-theoretic models agree on the barriers. They are confidentiality and privacy (including GDPR exposure), legal liability, reputational harm, missing trust, and free-riding ("consume only") behaviour. The privacy-preserving systems papers cite these same barriers as their motivation.

### Inferences

- Removing the victim's identity also removes the reputational benefit of being seen to contribute. Keyed, stable pseudonyms can restore a reputation signal without revealing who the contributor is, which matches the trust-taxonomy literature.

## Q3. Privacy-preserving CTI sharing techniques: anonymisation of logs and reports, k-anonymity/DP, MPC, homomorphic encryption, federated learning, blockchain, trust models

### Takeaway
Research has three strands. (1) Field-level sanitisation and anonymisation of alerts and logs (2004–2007), which attacks later showed to be fragile. (2) Cryptographic protocols (MPC, PSI, homomorphic and functional encryption) for computing over indicators or aggregating sightings without disclosure. (3) Since about 2019, federated learning and blockchain designs that share models or access-controlled records instead of data. Almost all of them protect IoCs, alerts or traffic features. None found shares sanitized incident *narratives* reduced to behaviour.

### Cited Findings

**Sanitising alerts and logs (seminal, pre-2015)**
- **P. Lincoln, P. Porras, V. Shmatikov, "Privacy-Preserving Sharing and Correlation of Security Alerts," 13th USENIX Security Symposium, 2004.** It proposes data-sanitisation techniques that allow community alert aggregation and correlation "while maintaining privacy for alert contributors". The approach "does not rely on trusted third parties or secure multiparty computation schemes, and does not require sophisticated key management" — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/13th-usenix-security-symposium/privacy-preserving-sharing-and-correlation-security-alerts); [PDF](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/sec04/tech/full_papers/lincoln/lincoln.pdf)
  - ATTACKDNA: the closest conceptual ancestor, since it also uses deterministic sanitisation to protect contributor privacy. It differs in operating on structured IDS alerts (IPs, ports) for correlation, whereas ATTACKDNA sanitises free-text incident reports and keeps only behaviour.
- **A. Slagell, K. Lakkaraju, K. Luo, "FLAIM: A Multi-level Anonymization Framework for Computer and Network Logs," USENIX LISA 2006.** A modular log anonymiser with multi-level anonymisation that lets administrators trade information loss against privacy — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/lisa-06/flaim-multi-level-anonymization-framework-computer-and-network-logs)
  - ATTACKDNA: FLAIM is policy-driven, field-level anonymisation of structured logs. ATTACKDNA borrows the idea of a deterministic, policy-driven sanitiser but applies it to text.

### Inferences
- The literature offers two privacy philosophies: compute on secrets (MPC, HE, FL) or publish non-secrets (sanitisation). ATTACKDNA belongs to the second. - I found no system that combines free-text de-identification of incident reports, reduction to an ATT&CK/CVE behaviour profile, and keyed contributor pseudonyms. TRADE covers anonymous accountability, Tikanmäki et al. cover anonymisation of CTI, and Ali et al. cover ATT&CK-structured sharing, but none does all three. This combination appears to be ATTACKDNA's defensible novelty (absence of evidence, not proof).

## Q4. Automated redaction and de-identification of free text (NER-based PII removal, Presidio, TAB) and the residual-leak problem

### Takeaway
NER- and regex-based de-identifiers such as Presidio are explicitly best-effort. Text-anonymisation research (TAB, re-identification attacks) shows that masking predefined entity types is not the same as preventing re-identification. LLMs can infer personal attributes from text that has already been anonymised. A deterministic sanitizer like ATTACKDNA's must therefore pair entity removal with *minimising what is kept*.

### Cited Findings
- **Microsoft Presidio (open-source PII detection and anonymisation).** "Because it is using automated detection mechanisms, there is no guarantee that Presidio will find all sensitive information. Consequently, additional systems and protections should be employed." Modules: analyzer (PII identification), anonymizer (operators), image redactor, structured. The documentation has moved from microsoft.github.io/presidio to data-privacy-stack.github.io/presidio (redirect observed Nov 2026) — [Presidio docs](https://data-privacy-stack.github.io/presidio/)
  - ATTACKDNA: Presidio is the natural baseline to compare against. ATTACKDNA's deterministic sanitizer targets security-specific identifiers (hostnames, internal paths, secrets, account names) that generic PII recognisers may miss. It should be evaluated with the same "no guarantee" honesty.
- **I. Pilán, P. Lison, L. Øvrelid, A. Papadopoulou, D. Sánchez, M. Batet, "The Text Anonymization Benchmark (TAB): A Dedicated Corpus and Evaluation Framework for Text Anonymization," Computational Linguistics 48(4):1053–1101, 2022, DOI 10.1162/coli_a_00458 (arXiv 2202.00443).** 1,268 English ECHR court cases annotated with semantic category, identifier type (direct or quasi-identifier), confidential attributes and co-reference. The corpus is designed to "go beyond traditional de-identification (which is limited to the detection of predefined semantic categories)" and comes with privacy and utility metrics — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1162/coli_a_00458); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00443)
  - ATTACKDNA: TAB's distinction between direct identifiers and quasi-identifiers suits evaluating ATTACKDNA. Hostnames and IPs are direct identifiers; sector, timing, a rare CVE combination or a unique toolset can act as quasi-identifiers of the victim.
- **P. Lison, I. Pilán, D. Sánchez, M. Batet, L. Øvrelid, "Anonymisation Models for Text Data: State of the art, Challenges and Future Directions," ACL-IJCNLP 2021 (Vol. 1), pp. 4188–4203, DOI 10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.323** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.323) (survey contrasting NLP de-identification with privacy-preserving data publishing; abstract not retrieved in full)
- **B. Manzanares-Salor, D. Sánchez, P. Lison, "Evaluating the disclosure risk of anonymized documents via a machine learning-based re-identification attack," Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery 38:4040–4075, 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10618-024-01066-3.** Recall against human annotation "is not a risk metric": it assumes a single ground truth, relies on subjective judgement, and weights all missed terms equally. The paper instead measures disclosure risk with an automated, language-model-based re-identification attack — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-024-01066-3)
  - ATTACKDNA: suggests an evaluation design. Train a classifier to guess the contributing organisation from stored Attack DNA records, and report that accuracy as residual risk, rather than only sanitizer recall.
- **R. Staab, M. Vero, M. Balunović, M. Vechev, "Beyond Memorization: Violating Privacy Via Inference with Large Language Models," ICLR 2024 (arXiv 2310.07298).** LLMs infer personal attributes from Reddit text with up to 85% top-1 and 95% top-3 accuracy, at about 100× lower cost and 240× less time than humans. "Common mitigations, i.e., text anonymization and model alignment, are currently ineffective" against LLM inference — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=kmn0BhQk7p)
  - ATTACKDNA: the strongest argument for storing *structured behaviour fields only* rather than sanitized prose. Residual narrative style and context is exactly what LLM inference exploits.
- **ENISA, "Pseudonymisation techniques and best practices," 3 Dec 2019.** Defines pseudonymisation actors and scenarios, analyses attacks on pseudonyms (brute force, dictionary search, guesswork) and presents the main techniques and policies — [ENISA](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/pseudonymisation-techniques-and-best-practices)
  - ATTACKDNA: a direct authority for its keyed pseudonyms. Unkeyed hashes of organisation names fall to dictionary attacks, which is why the key matters. **[The report's detailed treatment of keyed hash/HMAC was not quoted in my extract; confirm the section before citing it for HMAC specifically.]**
- **CTI-specific PII pipelines (lower confidence):** ExCyTIn-Bench (arXiv 2507.14201) is reported to anonymise PII in security logs with LLM passes followed by expert review — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14201) **[from a search summary; not read in full]**. A Brazilian SBRC paper on pseudonymising vulnerability-scan reports cites an earlier tool, AnonLFI, using a hybrid "NER + RegEx + SHA-256 (no salt)" approach — [SBC](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbrc_estendido/article/download/42580/42347/) **[from a search summary; authors and year unverified]**
  - ATTACKDNA: an unsalted SHA-256 is exactly the dictionary-attack weakness ENISA describes. ATTACKDNA's *keyed* pseudonyms are the correct contrast to draw.

### Inferences
- Treat the residual-leak problem in three layers. (a) Missed direct identifiers: sanitizer recall. (b) Quasi-identifiers in the kept fields: k-anonymity-style checks. (c) Inference from leftover prose: avoided by storing only structured fields. ATTACKDNA's design handles (c) by construction, but should measure (a) and (b).
- A deterministic, rule-based sanitizer is auditable and reproducible, and the same input always gives the same output. Its weakness is recall on novel identifier formats. A hybrid of deterministic rules plus an NER pass such as Presidio is a defensible future-work point.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve TAB's baseline numbers (for example, how off-the-shelf NER or Presidio scored on its privacy metric). They are in the paper body.
- I found no published benchmark for de-identifying *security incident reports* specifically, an analogue of TAB for CTI. This appears to be an open gap.

## Q5. Sharing TTP/behaviour-level intelligence instead of indicators, and anonymous, attribution-free sharing

### Takeaway
Practitioner and standards bodies (the Pyramid of Pain, MITRE ATT&CK, Attack Flow, the STIX Attack Pattern object, SAMA's "operational CTI") all value behaviour (TTPs) over atomic IoCs. Research has mostly worked on *extracting* TTPs from reports (TTPDrill and successors), not on *sharing them privately*. Anonymous sharing exists only as niche mechanisms: MISP delegated publishing, TRADE, and cryptographic sighting aggregation.

### Cited Findings
- **D. J. Bianco, "The Pyramid of Pain," Enterprise Detection & Response blog, 1 Mar 2013 (updated 17 Jan 2014).** It ranks indicator types by how much "pain it will cause them when you are able to deny those indicators": hash values, IP addresses, domain names, network/host artefacts, tools, and finally TTPs ("how the adversary goes about accomplishing their mission, from reconnaissance all the way through data exfiltration") — [blog](http://detect-respond.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-pyramid-of-pain.html)
  - ATTACKDNA: Attack DNA sits at the top of the pyramid (TTPs) plus CVEs. Bianco's ranking is the justification for discarding the bottom layers, which are also the most identifying (IPs, domains, hosts).
- **B. E. Strom, A. Applebaum, D. P. Miller, K. C. Nickels, A. G. Pennington, C. B. Thomas, "MITRE ATT&CK: Design and Philosophy," MITRE Product MP180360R1, July 2018, revised March 2020.** "A globally-accessible knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations" — [PDF](https://attack.mitre.org/docs/ATTACK_Design_and_Philosophy_March_2020.pdf)
  - ATTACKDNA: borrows ATT&CK as its controlled vocabulary for techniques.
- **MITRE Center for Threat-Informed Defense, "Attack Flow v4.0."** "A language for describing how cyber adversaries combine and sequence various offensive techniques to achieve their goals" — [Attack Flow](https://center-for-threat-informed-defense.github.io/attack-flow/)
  - ATTACKDNA: Attack Flow captures the *sequence* of techniques; ATTACKDNA currently stores a technique set plus vector and type. Attack Flow is a natural related-work contrast and possible future extension (ordered DNA).
- STIX 2.1 Attack Pattern SDO: a TTP object for "ways that adversaries attempt to compromise targets" — [STIX 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html)
- **G. Husari, E. Al-Shaer, M. Ahmed, B. Chu, X. Niu, "TTPDrill: Automatic and Accurate Extraction of Threat Actions from Unstructured Text of CTI Sources," ACSAC 2017, pp. 103–115, DOI 10.1145/3134600.3134646.** Without automated analytics of unstructured CTI text, "CTI sharing will be highly impractical" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3134600.3134646)
- **L. Li, C. Huang, J. Chen, "Automated discovery and mapping ATT&CK tactics and techniques for unstructured cyber threat intelligence," Computers & Security 140:103815, 2024, DOI 10.1016/j.cose.2024.103815** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2024.103815) (metadata only)
  - ATTACKDNA (TTPDrill and Li et al.): its technique extraction step belongs to this line of work. The difference is that ATTACKDNA extracts from the *victim's own* report and discards the source text, whereas extraction research works on public reports with no privacy goal.
- Ali et al. (2021) structure ledger-based private sharing around ATT&CK — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10092); TrustFed-CTI (2025) trains on "MITRE ATT&CK indicators" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/fi17110512)
- Bouwman et al.: commercial TI "is thought to provide unmatched coverage on attacker behavior", yet what they measured was mostly non-overlapping indicator feeds — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/bouwman)

**Anonymous / attribution-free sharing**
- MISP "delegated publishing … pseudo-anonymous sharing mechanism" — [MISP features](https://www.misp-project.org/features/)
- TRADE: organisations "preserve their anonymity while keeping organizations fully accountable" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13158)
- Lincoln et al. (2004): privacy "for alert contributors" in community aggregation — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/13th-usenix-security-symposium/privacy-preserving-sharing-and-correlation-security-alerts)
- van de Kamp et al. (2016): the aggregator learns total sightings per IoC "without learning the number of sightings from each individual party" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2994539.2994544)
- STIX 2.1 makes `created_by_ref` optional, so the standard allows anonymous objects — [STIX 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html)
  - ATTACKDNA: unlike one-off anonymous posting, keyed pseudonyms are *stable*. Members can tell that two incidents came from the same (unknown) contributor, which supports trust and reputation (compare Wagner et al. 2018) while keeping the contributor unidentified. The trade-off to acknowledge: a stable pseudonym lets observers link a contributor's records, and that linked profile can itself become a quasi-identifier.

### Inferences
- The "behaviour, not indicators" position is well supported by practitioner sources (Bianco, MITRE) and measurement studies (Li 2019, Bouwman 2020). ATTACKDNA's contribution is to tie it to privacy: the high-value layer of the pyramid is also the least identifying, so one design decision improves both utility and privacy.
- TTP-only records lose some value. They cannot be matched automatically the way IoCs can be blocked. ATTACKDNA should present itself as complementary to IoC platforms (MISP/AIS), not a replacement.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed empirical study measuring how identifying TTP sets are, i.e. how often a combination of ATT&CK techniques plus sector points to a single victim. This is a gap ATTACKDNA could fill.
- The rcATT paper (Legoy et al., "Automated Retrieval of ATT&CK Tactics and Techniques for Cyber Threat Reports," about 2020) was not resolved by Crossref, so it is omitted pending verification.

## Q6. Regional relevance: Saudi and GCC sharing frameworks (NCA and others)

### Takeaway
Saudi Arabia has a regulator-led, hub-and-spoke sharing model. The NCA monitors threats, issues alerts and reports, and runs an information-sharing platform on its national portal. A GCC-wide threat-information-sharing platform is being built with Saudi Information Technology Company (SITE), and SAMA has CTI Principles for the financial sector. I found no public evidence that any of these offer privacy-preserving or pseudonymous peer-to-peer incident sharing.

### Cited Findings
- NCA, "Cyber Threats and Incidents – Information Sharing and Threat Monitoring": "The Authority monitors cyber threats and attacks within the Kingdom's cyberspace, particularly those targeting national entities and critical infrastructure sectors. It prepares cybersecurity alerts and reports and shares them with the relevant entities" — [NCA](https://nca.gov.sa/en/cyber-operations/)
- NCA's national cybersecurity portal launched in 2022 with four platforms, including an Information-Sharing Platform described as enabling "real-time investigative information on local and global threats" — [TechAfricaNews, 2 Jun 2022](https://techafricanews.com/2022/06/02/saudi-nca-launches-national-portal-for-cybersecurity-services/) (secondary news source)
- The GCC Cybersecurity Executive Committee (under the GCC Ministerial Cybersecurity Committee, set up in 2021 on Saudi Arabia's proposal) discussed "progress on the cyber threat information-sharing platform for GCC countries, developed in cooperation with the Saudi Information Technology Company (SITE)" — [NCA news](https://nca.gov.sa/en/news/gcc-cybersecurity-executive-committee-holds-its-fifth-meeting/)
- **SAMA, "Cyber Threat Intelligence Principles" (financial sector, KSA).** Defines core, strategic, operational and technical/tactical CTI. *Operational* CTI covers "the modus operandi, behavior, and techniques used by threat actors"; all principles apply to SAMA member organisations — [SAMA Rulebook](https://rulebook.sama.gov.sa/en/entiresection/3087)
  - ATTACKDNA: SAMA's operational-CTI category is almost exactly ATTACKDNA's Attack DNA, which gives a local regulatory anchor for behaviour-level sharing in Saudi banking.
- NCA Essential Cybersecurity Controls (ECC-1:2018): 5 main domains, 29 subdomains, 114 controls — [ISC2, Jun 2025](https://www.isc2.org/Insights/2025/06/understanding-saudi-arabias-essential-cybersecurity-controls-and-workforce-framework). A vendor blog reports that ECC-2:2024 reduced this to 108 controls — [Secure.com blog](https://www.secure.com/blog/compliance/ecc-2-2024-july-2025-changes-gcc-security-program) **[vendor source; verify against NCA's official ECC-2:2024 text]**
- Historical note: a US Chamber of Commerce legal review based on the 2018 ECC said there was "no center or entity that is specifically designed for national threat information sharing" in Saudi Arabia. This is now outdated given the NCA portal — [US Chamber](https://www.uschamber.com/security/cybersecurity/international-cyber-law-project/middle-east)

### Inferences
- The Saudi and GCC model is regulator-led and centralised: entities report to and receive from the NCA. A sanitized, pseudonymous peer-sharing layer like ATTACKDNA could complement it by lowering the reputational cost of *voluntary* sharing between entities, alongside mandatory reporting to the regulator. This is the same tension Riesco et al. (2020) describe between obligations and voluntary exchange.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve NCA's official ECC or ECC-2:2024 PDF to quote the exact threat-intelligence and incident-management control numbers. A search summary pointed to an ECC control on threat intelligence feeds (2-13-3), but I did not verify it.
- I found no public technical documentation on the NCA information-sharing platform or the GCC platform: data model, anonymity guarantees, STIX use.
- I found no peer-reviewed study of CTI-sharing barriers specific to Saudi Arabia or the GCC.
