# Predicting an attacker's next steps (attack progression / TTP sequence prediction): related work for ATTACKDNA "Ask the memory"

Reference point used throughout. ATTACKDNA's prototype feature is record-based: it retrieves similar past incidents and shows the later kill-chain stages they reached, counted per incident, with example techniques. It does not predict. The prediction model that was evaluated and left out works like this. It sees the first half of 177 held-out ATT&CK profiles. In 56.5% of cases at least one of its 3 predictions is a step the attacker later took (hit@3). Guessing the most common steps scores 53.1%, so the gain is +3.4 points, about 6.4% relative. On 39 threat groups and campaigns it scores 66.7% against 59.0%, a gain of +7.7 points, about 13.1% relative. All "ATTACKDNA comparison" lines below are my inferences, not findings from the cited sources.

Verification note. Bibliographic data marked "(Crossref-verified)" was checked against the Crossref API during this session. Items marked "(not verified in session)" come from prior knowledge and should be checked before citing.

## Q1. Association and co-occurrence of ATT&CK techniques (Al-Shaer et al., TIE, follow-ups)

### Takeaway
The closest prior work treats ATT&CK techniques as unordered sets. It mines co-occurrence through clustering, association rules or recommender-style matrix factorisation, then infers techniques not yet observed from those already observed. MITRE CTID's Technique Inference Engine (TIE) is the best-documented system of this kind. Its gain over a popularity ("top techniques") baseline is modest: +3.3 recall points at k=10 and +6.2 at k=20, about 14–18% relative. That is the same order of magnitude as ATTACKDNA's +3.4 / +7.7 points, which makes TIE the strongest precedent for reporting a small gain honestly.

### Cited Findings
- **Al-Shaer, R., Spring, J. M., & Christou, E. (2020). "Learning the Associations of MITRE ATT&CK Adversarial Techniques." 2020 IEEE Conference on Communications and Network Security (CNS), pp. 1–9. DOI: 10.1109/CNS48642.2020.9162207** (Crossref-verified). An expanded preprint is arXiv:2005.01654 (v2, 12 May 2020), described as a "pre-print / expanded version of paper accepted for publication at IEEE CNS 2020". — [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/CNS48642.2020.9162207); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01654v2)
  - The authors apply statistical machine learning (hierarchical clustering) to the APT-group and Software data in ATT&CK. They infer technique associations "with 95% confidence". They report 98 technique associations (clusters) across APT and Software attacks, and 78% of the associated techniques show significant mutual information, which they read as "reasonably high predictability". The goal is to predict unobserved techniques from observed ones. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01654v2)
  - Caveat: one secondary listing (DeepAI) gives different clustering and MI figures. The 98 and 78% figures above come from the arXiv abstract. — [search result summary / DeepAI listing](https://deepai.org/publication/learning-the-associations-of-mitre-att-ck-adversarial-techniques)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): this is the same data source (ATT&CK group and software profiles) and the same order-free view. Al-Shaer et al. report association strength, not a held-out hit@k against a frequency baseline. ATTACKDNA's 56.5% vs 53.1% is therefore a stricter test of whether co-occurrence structure beats popularity.
- **Turner, M. J., Carenzo, M., Lasky, J., Morris-King, J., & Ross, J. (2025). "Technique Inference Engine: A Recommender Model to Support Cyber Threat Hunting." arXiv:2503.04819v1 (March 2025), MITRE Center for Threat-Informed Defense. No peer-reviewed venue is listed.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
  - Data: 43,899 technique observations across 6,236 CTI reports, covering 611 of 637 ATT&CK Enterprise v15.0 techniques (96%). Sources: OpenCTI (MITRE internal) 6,025 reports, ATT&CK Campaigns 22, TRAM 149, Attack Flows 29, Adversary Emulation Plans 10, participant-shared data 1. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
  - Models: Weighted Matrix Factorization (WMF, implicit feedback) and Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR). Protocol: 20% held out for test and 10% of training data for validation. Hyperparameters are tuned on validation recall@20. Results are averaged over 5 runs. The paper does not describe temporal ordering. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
  - Results:

    | Model | R@10 | R@20 | R@50 | NDCG@20 |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | TopTechniquesRecommender (popularity baseline) | 0.2285 | 0.3419 | 0.5646 | 0.1991 |
    | WMF | 0.2613 | 0.4037 | 0.6258 | 0.2232 |
    | BPR | 0.2382 | 0.4001 | 0.6579 | 0.2018 |

    The authors state that WMF beats the baseline by about 18% on recall@20 and about 12% on NDCG@20. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
  - Stated limitations: TTPs are "chronically underreported in CTI due to analyst bias". A missing label is ambiguous between absence and non-reporting. There is popularity bias: T1059 appears in 2,069 of 6,236 reports, about 33%, which favours the frequency baseline. Mislabeling may reduce fidelity. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
  - The CTID blog post frames TIE as a model that recommends likely TTPs from a known input TTP ("Know your adversary's next move with TIE", 9 Sept 2024). The GitHub project describes it as inferring associated ATT&CK techniques from previously observed ones. — [CTID blog](https://ctid.mitre.org/blog/2024/09/09/know-your-adversarys-next-move-with-tie/); [GitHub (via ecosyste.ms)](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fcenter-for-threat-informed-defense%2Ftechnique-inference-engine)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): TIE is the closest published analogue, and its gains over popularity are of similar size (relative +14% at R@10 and +18% at R@20, against ATTACKDNA's +6% and +13% relative). TIE's recall@k (the share of held-out techniques recovered) is not the same metric as ATTACKDNA's hit@3 (at least one of 3 correct), so only the size of the gap over popularity is comparable, not the absolute numbers. The blog's "next move" framing also overstates what an order-free recommender does, and ATTACKDNA's "record, not prediction" wording avoids that.
- **Rahman, M. R., & Williams, L. (2022). "Investigating co-occurrences of MITRE ATT&CK Techniques." arXiv:2211.06495 (11 Nov 2022), preprint.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06495)
  - Data: 115 cybercrime groups and 484 malware entries with their ATT&CK techniques. Methods: association rule mining and network analysis. Findings: T1059 (Command and Scripting Interpreter) is paired with many techniques, and T1082 (System Information Discovery) is used to decide the "next course of action". Defense Evasion and Discovery have the most techniques deployed. The authors derive 6 detection strategies, 6 mitigation strategies and 12 adversary behaviours. There is no predictive model and no formal ordering. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06495)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): this supports ATTACKDNA's finding that a few very common techniques (T1059, T1082) dominate the profiles, which is why the "most common steps" baseline is hard to beat.
- **Kuwano, M., Okuma, M., Okada, S., & Mitsunaga, T. (2023). "The Attacker Might Also Do Next: ATT&CK Behavior Forecasting by Attacker-based Collaborative Filtering and Graph Databases." Journal of Information Processing, 31, 802–811. DOI: 10.2197/ipsjjip.31.802.** An earlier version appeared at IEEE ICOCO 2022, pp. 191–197 (bibliographic detail from a search summary only). — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ipsjjip/31/0/31_802/_article/-char/en); [DBLP](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/jip/KuwanoOOM23.html)
  - A recommendation system based on collaborative filtering. If the observed attacker resembles an ATT&CK group, that group's other techniques are predicted to follow. It was evaluated on real-world attack cases with "higher recall than our previously proposed method". The landing page gives no numeric values and no frequency baseline. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ipsjjip/31/0/31_802/_article/-char/en)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): conceptually the nearest neighbour to "Ask the memory", which also finds similar past actors and shows what they did. Kuwano et al. call this "forecasting", whereas ATTACKDNA calls it a record. ATTACKDNA's explicit comparison with a popularity baseline is something this paper's abstract does not report.

### Inferences
- All Q1 methods model co-occurrence, not order. They answer "which other techniques does this kind of actor use", which is exactly what ATTACKDNA's record view shows. Any "next step" claim from them is ordering-agnostic.
- Popularity baselines are strong on ATT&CK data. TIE's baseline already reaches R@50 = 0.56, and WMF adds only 6 points. A small margin like ATTACKDNA's is therefore typical for this data, not a sign of a defective model.

### Gaps
- Exact Al-Shaer et al. dataset sizes (number of groups and software) and any held-out predictive accuracy were not visible in the abstract. The full PDF was not read.
- Kuwano et al. recall values were not retrieved, because the full-text PDF was not read.
- No peer-reviewed version of the TIE paper was found. The TIE GitHub evaluation notebook was not inspected.

## Q2. Sequence models: Markov/HMM, (Bayesian) attack graphs, RNN/transformer next-event models, KG link prediction, LLMs

### Takeaway
Sequence models report much higher numbers, such as 0.93 precision for Tiresias and 86% next-step accuracy in Raj et al. 2026. They do so on different data: dense, highly repetitive IPS or alert telemetry, or synthetic or derived sequences. Their baselines are usually other sequence models (Markov chains, n-grams), not a frequency baseline. Where a simple Markov baseline is reported it is competitive. KG link-prediction and LLM work on ATT&CK sequences reports modest absolute numbers (Hits@10 about 0.13–0.59, LLM multiple-choice tactic accuracy about 0.59). None of the ATT&CK-level work found reports a frequency baseline under a protocol like ATTACKDNA's.

### Cited Findings
**Surveys**
- **Husák, M., Komárková, J., Bou-Harb, E., & Čeleda, P. (2019). "Survey of Attack Projection, Prediction, and Forecasting in Cyber Security." IEEE Communications Surveys & Tutorials, 21(1), 640–660. DOI: 10.1109/COMST.2018.2871866** (Crossref-verified). The survey covers discrete models (attack graphs, Bayesian networks, Markov models), continuous models (time series, grey models), and machine learning and data mining. It focuses on "practical usability" and on "problems related to their evaluation". — [MUNI record](https://www.muni.cz/en/research/publications/1434138); [PDF](https://is.muni.cz/publication/1434138/2019-COMST-survey-of-attack-projection-prediction-forecasting.pdf)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): this is the standard survey to cite for the taxonomy. ATTACKDNA sits in a corner the survey barely covers, namely CTI-profile-level, case-based retrieval rather than network or alert-level projection.

**Markov / HMM and Bayesian attack graphs**
- **Holgado, P., Villagrá, V. A., & Vázquez, L. (2020). "Real-Time Multistep Attack Prediction Based on Hidden Markov Models." IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing, 17(1), 134–147. DOI: 10.1109/TDSC.2017.2751478** (Crossref-verified; online in 2017). HMM hidden states are the phases of a multistep attack, observations are IDS alerts, and prediction uses the Viterbi and forward-backward algorithms. Validation used a virtual DDoS scenario and showed real-time predictions are feasible. No top-k accuracy against a frequency baseline was found in the abstract. — [DBLP](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/rec/journals/tdsc/HolgadoVV20.html); [BVB record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_ieee_primary_8031986)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): this is a stage model like ATTACKDNA's kill-chain stages, but it is trained per scenario on ordered alerts. ATTACKDNA has no ordered alerts, only unordered CTI profiles.
- **Poolsappasit, N., Dewri, R., & Ray, I. (2012). "Dynamic Security Risk Management Using Bayesian Attack Graphs." IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing, 9(1), 61–74. DOI: 10.1109/TDSC.2011.34** (Crossref-verified; pre-2015, foundational). This is the standard Bayesian attack graph reference. Its content is described from prior knowledge: a BAG over vulnerability preconditions, updated with observed evidence (not verified in session). — [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TDSC.2011.34)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): BAGs need a network or vulnerability model of the victim. ATTACKDNA works only from an attack description, so BAGs belong to a different setting.
- **Nadeem, A., Verwer, S., Moskal, S., & Yang, S. J. (2022). "Alert-Driven Attack Graph Generation Using S-PDFA." IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing, 19(2), 731–746. DOI: 10.1109/TDSC.2021.3117348** (Crossref-verified DOI and authors; volume and pages from a search summary). Related: SAGE, the VizSec 2021 short paper (arXiv:2107.02783). A suffix-based probabilistic deterministic finite automaton (S-PDFA) learns attack graphs from intrusion alerts without expert knowledge. Over 330k alerts are compressed into 93 attack graphs (Collegiate Penetration Testing Competition data). Attackers "tend to follow shorter paths after they have discovered a longer one in 84.5% of the cases". — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:af89be21-a3a4-4701-be44-40f2c4677f63); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.02783v2)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): SAGE is also descriptive (summarising observed paths) rather than predictive, which mirrors ATTACKDNA's "record" framing at the alert level.

**Deep sequence models on security events and logs**
- **Shen, Y., Mariconti, E., Vervier, P.-A., & Stringhini, G. (2018). "Tiresias: Predicting Security Events Through Deep Learning." ACM CCS 2018, pp. 592–605. DOI: 10.1145/3243734.3243811** (Crossref-verified). — [arXiv/ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.10328); [PDF](https://seclab.bu.edu/papers/tiresias-ccs2018.pdf)
  - Data: 3.4 billion Symantec IPS events, about 740k machines, 27 days, 4,495 event types. Task: predict the next event on a machine (top-1). Results: precision 0.81–0.83 on 1–5 Nov, against a Markov chain at 0.52–0.62, a 3-gram at 0.54–0.67 and a Spectral method at 0.013–0.05. The best figure is up to 0.93 (Table 3 peak 0.935). Precision fell about 4% (0.83 to 0.79) over days, and retraining takes about 10 hours. Stated limitations: rare events may be mispredicted, the model only knows events it was trained on, the data is passive (signature-matched only), and the dataset comes from a single vendor. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.10328)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): the large margin of about 16–29 points over Markov and n-gram baselines comes from billions of densely ordered events. ATTACKDNA has hundreds of unordered profiles, so a Tiresias-sized gap should not be expected. Tiresias also did not compare against a pure popularity baseline.
- **Li, T., Jiang, Y., Lin, C., Obaidat, M. S., Shen, Y., & Ma, J. (2023). "DeepAG: Attack Graph Construction and Threats Prediction With Bi-Directional Deep Learning." IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing, 20(1), 740–757. DOI: 10.1109/TDSC.2022.3143551** (Crossref-verified). A transformer detects APT sequences in system logs, and a forward plus backward LSTM predicts attack paths. It adds an out-of-vocabulary processor and online updates. Reported: more than 99% of over 15,000 sequences detected, and the baseline improved "by 11.166% of accuracy" in prediction. Datasets are generic logs (HDFS, OpenStack, PageRank, BGL), not ATT&CK. — [AMiner](https://www.aminer.org/pub/622a53aa5aee126c0fe0a101/deepag-attack-graph-construction-and-threats-prediction-with-bi-directional-deep-learning); [researchr](https://researchr.org/publication/LiJLOSM23/bibtex)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): the log-anomaly benchmarks are unrelated to CTI technique profiles, and the 11.166% gain is over a learned baseline, not over frequency.
- **van Ede, T., Aghakhani, H., Spahn, N., Bortolameotti, R., Cova, M., Continella, A., van Steen, M., Peter, A., Kruegel, C., & Vigna, G. (2022). "DEEPCASE: Semi-Supervised Contextual Analysis of Security Events." IEEE S&P 2022, pp. 522–539. DOI: 10.1109/SP46214.2022.9833671** (Crossref-verified bibliographic data only). It analyses the context (preceding events) of security events with attention. The content summary is from prior knowledge (not verified in session). The same first author's GitHub account hosts a public PyTorch re-implementation of Tiresias. — [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP46214.2022.9833671); [Tiresias re-implementation](https://github.com/Thijsvanede/Tiresias)
- **Shen, Y., & Stringhini, G. (2019). "ATTACK2VEC: Leveraging Temporal Word Embeddings to Understand the Evolution of Cyberattacks." **ATT&CK-level sequence models (transformer / LSTM / Markov)**
- **Zhang, S., Xue, X., & Su, X. (2025). "DeepOP: A Hybrid Framework for MITRE ATT&CK Sequence Prediction via Deep Learning and Ontology." Electronics, 14(2), 257. DOI: 10.3390/electronics14020257** (Crossref-verified). The framework extracts causally connected attack events from CTI reports with an ontology (COASE), maps them to ATT&CK, and predicts with a transformer using "causal window self-attention". The abstract claims only "competitive performance in multi-step attack prediction"; no numbers were retrieved (the MDPI page returned 403). — [Crossref abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics14020257); [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2079-9292/14/2/257)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): DeepOP addresses the missing-ordering problem by extracting ordered sequences from report text. That is a possible next step for ATTACKDNA, but DeepOP's gain over a frequency baseline is unknown.
- **Raj, M., Bastian, N. D., Fiondella, L., & Kul, G. (2026). "MITRE ATT&CK-based Attack Chain Prediction Using Hybrid LSTM-Markov Models for Cyber Risk Assessment." Proc. 23rd Int. This is anecdotal and not peer reviewed. — [GitHub](https://github.com/PUGALENTHI-K-7/MITRE-ATTACK-Early-Forecasting)

**Knowledge-graph link prediction**
- **Kaiser, F. K., Dardik, U., Elitzur, A., Zilberman, P., Daniel, N., Wiens, M., Schultmann, F., Elovici, Y., & Puzis, R. (2023). DOI: 10.1109/TDSC.2022.3233703** (Crossref-verified). AttackDB is a multi-level threat knowledge base linking ATT&CK techniques to low-level telemetry.   - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): the paper infers co-present techniques from artifacts (a hypothesis set), not future steps. Its MAP of about 0.5 is a ranking metric not directly comparable to hit@3.
- **Xiang, X., Ma, C., Zeng, L., Feng, W., Xie, Y., & Gu, Z. (2025). "Uncovering multi-step attacks with threat knowledge graph reasoning." Security and Safety, 4, 2024019. DOI: 10.1051/sands/2024019** (from the PDF text). — [PDF](https://sands.edpsciences.org/articles/sands/pdf/2025/01/sands20240007.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1051/sands/2024019)

The columns appear to be swapped or wrong, so these numbers should be cited with that caveat. — [PDF, Table 8](https://sands.edpsciences.org/articles/sands/pdf/2025/01/sands20240007.pdf)

**LLM-based work**
- **Ma, H., Yong, J., Ma, Y., Chen, K., Yusof, A., Liang, Z., & Chang, E.-C. (2025/2026). "AttackSeqBench: Benchmarking the Capabilities of LLMs for Attack Sequences Understanding." arXiv:2503.03170 (v1 5 Mar 2025; v3 3 Mar 2026), preprint.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03170); [HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03170v3)
  - Built from 408 CTI reports (e.g., Cisco Talos, Microsoft). Tasks: Tactic and Technique are multiple-choice, inferring a hidden step in an attack sequence; Procedure is yes/no. The task of guessing the attacker's next stage is hard even for humans, which supports ATTACKDNA keeping the model out of the prototype.
- No peer-reviewed LLM system was found that predicts the next ATT&CK technique for an ongoing incident and reports a frequency baseline. LLM work is mostly about mapping text or logs to TTPs. — [search summary covering e.g. arXiv:2401.00280 and arXiv:2306.14062](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.00280)

### Inferences
- The impressive numbers in the literature (0.93, 86%, >99%) come from dense ordered telemetry, from derived or synthetic sequences, or from metrics that are not stated. Comparisons are against sequence baselines (Markov, n-gram), not popularity. They should not be held up as the bar for ATTACKDNA's task.
- At the ATT&CK and CTI level, where data looks like ATTACKDNA's, published numbers are modest: TIE R@10 = 0.26, A4CKGE multi-step MRR = 0.29, LLM tactic accuracy about 0.59.

### Gaps
- DeepOP's quantitative results were not retrieved (MDPI returned 403).
- Holgado et al.'s quantitative accuracy was not visible in the abstract.
- Kaiser et al.'s MAP and AUC values were not confirmed from full text.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of an LLM forecasting the next technique against a popularity baseline was found (as of this search).

## Q3. Kill-chain / Unified Kill Chain modelling and campaign-level progression

### Takeaway
Kill-chain models (Lockheed Martin 2011, Pols' 18-phase Unified Kill Chain 2017) give the stage vocabulary ATTACKDNA uses for "later stages". Recent ML work that "predicts kill-chain phases" often classifies techniques into phases rather than forecasting progression. MITRE's own response to the lack of sequences in ATT&CK is Attack Flow, a separate representation of ordered technique flows.

### Cited Findings
- **Hutchins, E. M., Cloppert, M. J., & Amin, R. M. (2011). "Intelligence-Driven Computer Network Defense Informed by Analysis of Adversary Campaigns and Intrusion Kill Chains." Proc. 6th Int. Conf. on Information Warfare and Security (ICIW), Lockheed Martin white paper** (pre-2015, foundational; not verified in session, as the Crossref query returned an unrelated item). — [Lockheed Martin PDF (unverified URL)](https://www.lockheedmartin.com/content/dam/lockheed-martin/rms/documents/cyber/LM-White-Paper-Intel-Driven-Defense.pdf)
- **Pols, P. (2017). "The Unified Kill Chain." White paper, developed with Fox-IT and Leiden University (dated 7 Dec 2017 in one source).** The model has 18 attack phases and combines the cyber kill chain with MITRE ATT&CK. Secondary sources mention later updates (2021/2022), with inconsistent dates. The "In / Through / Out" grouping is unverified. — [Wikipedia: Cyber kill chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber_kill_chain); [unifiedkillchain.com (primary, not fetched)](https://www.unifiedkillchain.com/)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): ATTACKDNA's per-incident stage counts are a descriptive use of a kill-chain ordering imposed on unordered technique sets. This is defensible when presented as "stages reached", but the stage order comes from the model, not from observed timing.
- **MITRE CTID Attack Flow.** Problem statement: "Tracking adversary behaviors one action at a time makes it hard to build effective defenses against multi-phased attacks." Solution: "Create a language, and associated tooling, to describe flows of ATT&CK techniques." The project page lists a release dated 30 July 2026, likely a later version, since the project predates 2026 (the start date was not verified). TIE's training corpus includes 29 Attack Flows. — [CTID Attack Flow](https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/attack-flow/); [TIE paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): Attack Flows are the only MITRE-curated ordered data, and they are tiny (29 in TIE's corpus). This is evidence that order-aware prediction on ATT&CK lacks training data.
- **Singh, C., Dhanraj, M., & Huang, K. (2025). "KillChainGraph: ML Framework for Predicting and Mapping ATT&CK Techniques." arXiv:2508.18230 (19 Aug 2025), preprint.** ATTACK-BERT maps techniques to the 7 Cyber Kill Chain phases. The models are LightGBM, a Transformer, BERT and a GNN, combined in a weighted soft-voting ensemble. Ensemble F1 is 97.47–99.83% across phases, against GNN 97.36–99.81%. The data is ATT&CK Enterprise. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18230)
  - ATTACKDNA comparison (inference): the near-perfect F1 is for assigning techniques to phases, which is largely determined by ATT&CK's own tactic labels. It is not forecasting what an attacker does next. It is a useful example of why headline numbers must be read against the task.
- Raj et al. (SECRYPT 2026) build campaign-level chains: 4,849 chains, but from only 33 independent campaigns. — [SciTePress](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2026/150754/)

### Inferences
- Campaign-level progression analysis in the literature is mostly descriptive (SAGE, Attack Flow) or uses chains derived from a few dozen campaigns. ATTACKDNA's 39 groups and campaigns is a comparable evidence base, and its record-not-prediction framing matches the descriptive strand.

### Gaps
- The primary UKC white paper and Hutchins et al. were not fetched, so their bibliographic details are unverified.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that evaluates UKC-stage forecasting from CTI profiles against a popularity baseline.

## Q4. Reported accuracies, datasets, baselines: how strong are gains over frequency baselines?

### Takeaway
Only one ATT&CK-level work found (TIE) reports an explicit popularity baseline under held-out evaluation. Its best model gains 3–9 recall points (about 11–18% relative) depending on k, comparable to ATTACKDNA's +3.4 / +7.7 points (6% / 13% relative). Papers with much bigger numbers either compare against weaker or sequence baselines, use telemetry rather than CTI, or do not state the metric or baseline.

- For CTI-profile data, typical gains over popularity are small: single-digit percentage points, or about 10–20% relative. ATTACKDNA's result sits at the lower end at 6% relative on all profiles, and within the typical range at 13% relative on groups and campaigns.

- Recommended framing: "consistent with the only comparable published evaluation (TIE), gains over popularity on ATT&CK data are modest".

### Gaps

- No study was found that reports hit@3 on half-profile completion of ATT&CK groups, the exact ATTACKDNA protocol.

## Q5. Known limitations: data sparsity, CTI reporting bias, no ordering in ATT&CK profiles, evaluation leakage

### Takeaway
The literature's own limitations line up closely with ATTACKDNA's reasons for keeping the model out of the prototype. These are underreporting and analyst bias in CTI, popularity bias, rare-event failure, concept drift, scarce ordered data, and small numbers of independent campaigns behind large derived datasets, which creates leakage risk.

### Cited Findings
- **Reporting bias and sparsity.** "TTPs are chronically underreported in CTI due to analyst bias". A missing technique may be absent or simply unreported, and mislabeling may reduce fidelity. — [TIE](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
- **Popularity bias.** T1059 in 2,069 of 6,236 reports inflates the popularity baseline and favours popular items. — [TIE](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
- **Hub techniques.** T1059 co-occurs with many techniques, and Defense Evasion and Discovery dominate. — [Rahman & Williams 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06495)
- **Rare events and drift.** Tiresias "may not correctly predict these rare intrusion attempts", can only recognise trained events, and lost about 4% precision over days. A December discontinuity was linked to patching. — [Tiresias](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.10328)
- **Rare or edge tactics for LLMs.** Tactic inference is the hardest task, for humans too (0.51). Some tactics occur mainly at the start or end of sequences. — [AttackSeqBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03170v3)
- **Ordering not recorded.** MITRE created Attack Flow because "tracking adversary behaviors one action at a time makes it hard to build effective defenses against multi-phased attacks". The ATT&CK-based association and recommender methods (Al-Shaer et al., Rahman & Williams, TIE) all treat techniques as sets. — [Attack Flow](https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/attack-flow/); [Al-Shaer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01654v2); [TIE](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04819v1)
- **Few independent units / leakage risk.** 4,849 chains derive from only 33 independent campaigns. — [Raj et al.](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2026/150754/)
- **Synthetic sequences.** Multi-step training data generated by ChatGPT-4, a random triple split, and internally inconsistent Hits@k. — [Xiang et al.](https://sands.edpsciences.org/articles/sands/pdf/2025/01/sands20240007.pdf)
- **Evaluation practice.** Husák et al. explicitly flag "problems related to their evaluation" as a focus of their survey. — [Husák et al.](https://www.muni.cz/en/research/publications/1434138)
- **Single-vendor, passive data.** Tiresias uses only signature-matched events from one vendor and compares only against simple baselines. — [Tiresias](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.10328)

### Inferences
- **Ordering.** ATT&CK group and software technique lists are unordered sets, so "first half of the profile" in ATTACKDNA's evaluation is not a temporal first half. It is presumably a kill-chain-ordered or arbitrary split. The report should state which, because "predicting later steps" then partly means "predicting later-stage tactics", which favours stage-aware baselines.
- **Leakage.** In ATTACKDNA, software profiles and group profiles in ATT&CK share techniques: a group's profile partly aggregates its software. Campaigns are also linked to groups. A random held-out split can therefore put near-duplicates in train and test, which can inflate both the model and kNN retrieval. Splitting by group or by time would be more conservative.
- **Reporting bias.** Bias affects the "record" too. Later stages (Exfiltration, Impact) may be under-reported in public CTI, so stage counts per incident describe what was reported, not what happened. ATTACKDNA's "record, not prediction" wording should say "reported".
- **Positioning.** The modest 56.5% vs 53.1% gain matches TIE's modest gain over popularity on similar data. Keeping prediction out of the prototype is consistent with the literature's own limitations.

### Gaps
- No primary MITRE documentation was retrieved that explicitly states that group or software technique lists carry no temporal order. This is inferred from Attack Flow's rationale and the set-based designs above.
- No paper was found that quantifies leakage between ATT&CK software and group profiles in a prediction setting.
- Reporting bias by tactic (e.g., under-reporting of later stages) was not quantified in any source retrieved here.
