# ATTACKDNA separates attacker behaviour from victim identity

## 1. Abstract

ATTACKDNA is a prototype cybersecurity platform that links organisations through a shared memory of attacks. When one organisation adds an incident report, the platform strips the victim's identity, keeps the attacker's behaviour as an "Attack DNA" fingerprint mapped to MITRE ATT&CK and the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog, and lets other organisations recall it during an attack ("Ask the memory") or after one ("Analyze a report"). The prototype runs end to end, offline, on one machine. On data it was not tuned on, it removed **275 of 279** hand-marked identifiers (98.6%), named the attack type correctly in **24 of 28** real incident summaries taken from Wikipedia and used only for testing, and put organisation A's incident in organisation B's top five results in **19 of 20** attacks while naming A on **0 of 20** screens ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). These figures are indicative, not conclusive: the samples are small (the 95% interval for "B finds A's incident first" runs from 34% to 74%), the cross-organisation reports are synthetic and were written and labelled by the team, and no real SOC report and no LLM-mode run has been evaluated. The multi-organisation platform is simulated inside one installation without accounts or access control, and prediction of the attacker's next step exists only on an unmerged branch. Across four strands of related work, we found no reviewed system that combines free-text de-identification, reduction of an incident to ATT&CK/CVE behaviour, keyed contributor pseudonyms and cross-organisation recall of responses labelled by source and effectiveness. That combination is the project's defensible contribution; the weak point is the evidence, not the idea.

## 2. Introduction: the second attack should be a lookup

Security teams routinely face attacks that another organisation has already contained, and they almost never learn from that organisation's experience. The ATTACKDNA repository puts the problem in one line: "Every organisation solves the same attack twice. The first time it is an incident; the second time it should be a lookup. It usually isn't, because the useful part of an incident report is buried inside data nobody is allowed to share" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The guidance incident responders follow already asks for this kind of reuse. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025) moves lessons learned out of a final lifecycle phase and into continuous improvement under CSF 2.0 (ID.IM). It states that lessons "should often be shared as soon as they are identified, not delayed until after recovery concludes", describes reviews as "an opportunity to review what happened, what actions were taken, and how effective those actions were", and lists "identifying TTPs that are not currently being blocked by safeguards" among the improvements ([NIST SP 800-61r3](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-61r3.pdf)). In practice the loop rarely closes: research on case-based incident handling notes that resolution knowledge is "being under-used… sometimes even lost and not used at all" ([Nunes et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1142/S021819401940014X)).

The stakes come from how much attacker behaviour repeats. A 2026 measurement of ATT&CK's public knowledge base found that **only 34% of threat groups have any group-specific technique**, and that 64% still have no group-specific behaviour after CVEs and report-extracted techniques are added ([Saha et al., ASIA CCS 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10645)). Behaviour is shared widely across attackers, so a response that stopped a technique chain at one organisation is likely to matter at many others. The repository itself does not quantify how often attacks recur across organisations; its motivation is stated, not measured.

The project also fits its region. In Saudi Arabia, the National Cybersecurity Authority (NCA) monitors threats against national entities and critical infrastructure, and it "prepares cybersecurity alerts and reports and shares them with the relevant entities" ([NCA](https://nca.gov.sa/en/cyber-operations/)). A GCC-wide threat-information-sharing platform is being built with the Saudi Information Technology Company (SITE) ([NCA news](https://nca.gov.sa/en/news/gcc-cybersecurity-executive-committee-holds-its-fifth-meeting/)). SAMA's Cyber Threat Intelligence Principles for the financial sector define *operational* CTI as "the modus operandi, behavior, and techniques used by threat actors" ([SAMA Rulebook](https://rulebook.sama.gov.sa/en/entiresection/3087)), which is almost exactly what ATTACKDNA calls Attack DNA. We found no public evidence that these national or regional channels offer privacy-preserving, pseudonymous incident sharing between peer organisations.

ATTACKDNA was developed by students of the College of Computer and Information Sciences at King Saud University ([poster](./ATTACKDNA_SAIF_Poster_SAIF130191.pdf)), and the README lists Aryam Al-Qahtani and Norah Almishdaq as the team ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). This report describes the project as it stands in its repository at commit `81b0378`; later commits add only documents. Every figure is copied from committed result files, not re-measured. The report then places the project against four strands of related research and closes with its limitations and a path to the global platform the team envisions.

## 3. Problem Statement: lessons stay locked in reports nobody may share

The first obstacle is that incident reports identify their victims. Surveys and models of information sharing agree on the barriers: confidentiality and privacy, legal liability, reputational damage, lack of trust and free-riding ([Wagner et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2019.101589); [Skopik et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2016.04.003)), and practitioner surveys test those barriers against what security managers and analysts actually report ([Zibak & Simpson 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3339252.3340528)). Organisations hold back because of "the risk of incidentally disclosing sensitive business data or personally identifiable information, and the subsequent reputational harm or even financial repercussions imposed by the GDPR" ([Preuveneers & Joosen 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3538969.3538982)). IP addresses and account names inside CTI can themselves be personal data under the GDPR ([Albakri et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-21752-5_3)). Collaborative schemes "are rarely put into practice owing to confidentiality, trust, and liability challenges" ([Freudiger et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-20550-2_17)), and participants "are often reluctant to share their CTI and prefer to consume only" ([Riesco et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11235-019-00613-4)). Game-theoretic work models the same incentive problem ([Gal-Or & Ghose 2005](https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.1050.0053); [Garrido-Pelaz et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2994539.2994543)).

The second obstacle is that what does get shared is mostly indicators, protected by access control rather than by removing the victim from the content. MISP is "a trusted platform that allows the collection and sharing of important indicators of compromise (IoC) of targeted attacks" ([Wagner et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2994539.2994542)), and its privacy comes from distribution levels and sharing groups ([MISP features](https://www.misp-project.org/features/)). CISA's Automated Indicator Sharing (AIS) exchanges "machine-readable cyber threat indicators and defensive measures" among PKI-certified participants and relies on the legal protections of the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 ([CISA AIS](https://www.cisa.gov/topics/cyber-threats-and-advisories/information-sharing/automated-indicator-sharing-ais)). That model is losing participants. The DHS Inspector General found that **indicator sharing through AIS fell 93% from 2020 to 2022**, and that the number of participants sharing fell to its lowest level since 2017 ([OIG-24-60](https://oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/2024-09/OIG-24-60-Sep24.pdf)). Users said the main problem was "the quality of data they receive back from the system" ([SC World](https://www.scworld.com/news/companies-that-opt-out-of-dhs-threat-sharing-platform-call-for-better-data)), although SANS objected that the OIG did not evaluate AIS's impact on prevention ([SANS](https://www.sans.org/blog/reflections-on-the-us-governments-oig-report-on-cisas-automated-indicator-sharing-program)). The legal shield itself keeps expiring. The 2015 Act's protections lapsed in autumn 2025, were revived to 30 January 2026 and were then extended to 30 September 2026 ([Hunton](https://hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/congress-extends-cybersecurity-information-sharing-act-of-2015-through-september-2026)), with a further move to 11 December 2026 pending ([CRS IF12959](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-03_IF12959_e88b818ccb3aca205dcf1ba233b57cd55207205b.html)). If the Act expires, its FOIA exemptions, liability limits and privilege protections go with it ([CNAS](https://www.cnas.org/publications/cnas-insights/cnas-insights-the-case-for-long-term-cisa-2015-reauthorization)).

The third obstacle is that indicators are low-value and short-lived. Two leading commercial vendors' indicators show "almost no overlap": for 22 actors that both claim to track, **overlap averages 2.5–4.0%**, and shared indicators appear in the other feed about a month later ([Bouwman et al. 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/bouwman)). A comparative study of public and commercial feeds found "significant limitations and challenges in using existing threat intelligence data for its purported goals" ([Li et al. 2019](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/li)). Bianco's Pyramid of Pain ranks hashes, IP addresses and domains at the bottom and TTPs at the top, because denying an attacker its behaviour hurts most ([Bianco 2013](http://detect-respond.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-pyramid-of-pain.html)). Sharing platforms "often only provide threat data instead of the promised actionable intelligence" ([Sauerwein et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3465481.3470048)). The main exchange standard has no home for a sanitized incident either: in STIX 2.1, "the Incident object … is a stub" that implementers must extend ([OASIS STIX 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html)), and existing incident-reporting formats differ widely ([Menges & Pernul 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2017.10.009)). What responders most need, in NIST's words what actions were taken and how effective they were, is carried by none of these feeds.

The fourth obstacle is that deleting names is not anonymity. Anonymised network traces still leak sensitive information through inference ([Coull et al. 2007](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/ndss2007/playing-devils-advocate-inferring-sensitive-information-anonymized-network-traces/)). Microsoft's Presidio warns that "there is no guarantee that Presidio will find all sensitive information" ([Presidio](https://data-privacy-stack.github.io/presidio/)). Text-anonymisation research separates direct identifiers from the quasi-identifiers a de-identifier can leave behind ([Pilán et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1162/coli_a_00458)). LLMs infer personal attributes from text with up to 85% top-1 accuracy, and "text anonymization and model alignment, are currently ineffective" against such inference ([Staab et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298)).

Put together, the problem ATTACKDNA addresses is this: **how can one organisation reuse another's incident experience (the attacker's behaviour and the response that worked) without learning, or being able to infer, who the victim was?**

## 4. Objectives: five measurable targets

The poster states three objectives. The first is to remove the victim's identifiers before anything is stored or shared, measured on hand-marked text. The second is to map a free-text report to ATT&CK techniques, tested on data the system was not tuned on. The third is to let another organisation recall the same attack and what stopped it without learning who was hit ([poster](./ATTACKDNA_SAIF_Poster_SAIF130191.pdf)). The repository's evaluation scripts and tests imply two more: keep the attack's behaviour intact through redaction, and run offline at interactive speed. The table states each objective with the metric the project uses and its measured status; section 8 discusses each one.

## 5. Proposed Solution: a shared memory that keeps the attack and forgets the victim

ATTACKDNA's answer is to separate the attack from the victim. In the README's words, it "strips the victim's identity out of an incident report and keeps the attack's *behaviour*, its **Attack DNA**, as a searchable record. With the security team's approval, that fingerprint joins a **shared memory**: the next organisation hit by the same pattern recalls it, sees why it matched and what worked, and never learns who was attacked" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The code defines Attack DNA as "the reusable, victim-free fingerprint of an incident: what kind of attack it was, which ATT&CK behaviours it used, which known-exploited vulnerabilities were involved, what classes of indicator appeared, and what the business impact was" ([dna_extractor.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)). The name follows from that definition. DNA identifies an organism wherever a sample is found; the fingerprint identifies an attack pattern whichever organisation it hit. As the same source puts it, "two incidents at two different organisations that share a DNA signature are comparable — which is what makes a past incident's response reusable today".

A contribution follows a fixed path. Organisation A attaches its report, a deterministic privacy layer removes identifiers, and the system extracts the Attack DNA. It shows A what was removed, what DNA was found and what memory already holds. The incident is stored only if A's team turns on the **Add to memory** switch: "Turning it on is the team's approval; a report that fails the privacy check is never added" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). Even then, only sanitized text, the DNA and the team's sanitized response actions are stored. The organisation is kept as a keyed pseudonym, the share month replaces the incident date, and the raw report is never stored ([analyze.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py)). The victim's sector is deliberately left out: "Once names are removed, the sector is itself a clue to who the victim was, so it is neither inferred nor shared, and matching uses behaviour alone" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)).

Another organisation, B, uses the memory in two modes. The table summarises them, and the poster's worked examples show both ([poster](./ATTACKDNA_SAIF_Poster_SAIF130191.pdf)).

| Mode | When | What B provides | What B gets back |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ask the memory** | During an ongoing attack | A description of what the attacker has done so far and how they got in | (1) Actions that worked against similar past incidents; (2) past attackers who got in the same way, and what they did; (3) what attackers did next in those incidents, counted as a record rather than predicted |
| **Analyze a report** | After the attack | The incident report (txt, md, log, pdf or docx) | (1) Identifiers removed, with counts per category; (2) the Attack DNA with ATT&CK evidence; (3) similar attacks in memory (another organisation's are marked *identity withheld*), why they match, what was done and how well it worked; (4) what to do if this attack is met again, each action with its source |

Several design commitments run through both modes. Redaction is done "with rules and word lists, not a model. A rule cannot decide to leak an IP address, and the same report always gives the same output" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The optional LLM only ever sees text that has passed the privacy check. Shared memory holds behaviour, not indicators: hashes, CVEs and registry keys are kept, while addresses, domains, mailboxes, hosts and accounts are removed and only their shape survives ("3 distinct network addresses, 1 mailbox"). Every match carries its reasons, and every recommended action carries its source. "Nothing is trained on anyone's incidents"; the one learning component learns only from sentences MITRE publishes. "What came next" is presented as "a record of what happened in similar past incidents, not a prediction" ([guidance.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/guidance.py)). The longer-term vision, stated in the README's next steps and on the poster, is a multi-organisation deployment "towards a global platform" ([poster](./ATTACKDNA_SAIF_Poster_SAIF130191.pdf)).

## 6. Related Work: four strands, one unoccupied combination

ATTACKDNA sits where four research strands meet: privacy-preserving sharing of threat intelligence between organisations; mapping incident and CTI text to MITRE ATT&CK; retrieval of similar incidents and recommendation of responses, including LLM and RAG assistants; and prediction of the attacker's next step. Each subsection ends with how ATTACKDNA differs, and one comparison table closes the section.

### 6.1 Privacy-preserving sharing protects who can see the data, not what it reveals

Production platforms and standards exchange indicators and STIX objects among identified members and control *who can see* them. MISP offers distribution levels, sharing groups "down to individual attributes", signing and encryption, and an opt-in "delegated publishing … through a simple, pseudo-anonymous sharing mechanism" in which a trusted community republishes on a member's behalf ([MISP features](https://www.misp-project.org/features/)). OpenCTI organises CTI as a knowledge graph with data segregation and authorised members ([OpenCTI docs](https://docs.opencti.io/latest/)). STIX 2.1 defines 18 domain objects, Attack Pattern and Vulnerability among them, and makes the author reference `created_by_ref` optional; TAXII 2.1 transports them ([STIX 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/stix/v2.1/os/stix-v2.1-os.html); [TAXII 2.1](https://docs.oasis-open.org/cti/taxii/v2.1/os/taxii-v2.1-os.html)). NIST and ENISA publish guidance on sharing communities and platforms ([NIST SP 800-150](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-150); [ENISA 2018a](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/information-sharing-and-analysis-center-isacs-cooperative-models); [ENISA 2018b](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/exploring-the-opportunities-and-limitations-of-current-threat-intelligence-platforms)). A comparison of 22 platforms found no common definition of a sharing platform and little empirical research ([Sauerwein et al. 2017](https://aisel.aisnet.org/wi2017/track08/paper/3/)). A 2022 survey of 69 experts in Germany, Austria and Switzerland found interest in such platforms growing ([Fischer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3600160.3600185)).

Research systems follow three privacy philosophies. The first sanitises data before release. Lincoln, Porras and Shmatikov sanitised IDS alerts so that a community could correlate them "while maintaining privacy for alert contributors", without trusted third parties or secure multiparty computation ([Lincoln et al. 2004](https://www.usenix.org/conference/13th-usenix-security-symposium/privacy-preserving-sharing-and-correlation-security-alerts)). FLAIM offered multi-level, policy-driven log anonymisation ([Slagell et al. 2006](https://www.usenix.org/conference/lisa-06/flaim-multi-level-anonymization-framework-computer-and-network-logs)). Prefix-preserving IP anonymisation made keyed, deterministic pseudonymisation of identifiers standard practice ([Xu et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICNP.2002.1181415); [Fan et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comnet.2004.03.033)). Sadique et al. generated STIX documents from firewall logs, malware signatures and spam while preserving privacy ([Sadique et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/UEMCON.2018.8796822)). The second philosophy computes on secrets. SEPIA aggregated network events across domains with MPC for up to 140 input providers ([Burkhart et al. 2010](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/sec10/tech/full_papers/Burkhart.pdf)). Controlled data sharing improved predictive blacklisting accuracy by up to 105% on 2 billion DShield IP addresses ([Freudiger et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-20550-2_17)), although a later study found that naive collaboration "also yields high false positives" and proposed a semi-trusted central entity ([Melis et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3310165.3310168)). Private IoC sightings let an aggregator learn totals without learning each party's counts ([van de Kamp et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2994539.2994544)). PRACIS combined format-preserving and homomorphic encryption over STIX for an untrusted broker, at up to 689 incidents per minute ([de Fuentes et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2016.12.011)), and TATIS used attribute-based encryption to control access to threat-intelligence APIs ([Preuveneers & Joosen 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-45371-8_11)). The third philosophy shares models or ledgers instead of data. Examples are federated anomaly detection for IoT ([Nguyen et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07474)), federated NetFlow IDS training across organisations ([Sarhan et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10922-022-09691-3)), and TrustFed-CTI's federated learning with reputation scores, differential privacy and MPC ([Mrabet 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/fi17110512)). Ledger designs provide sharing incentives ([Riesco et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11235-019-00613-4); [Menges et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10207-020-00528-1)), exchange that is anonymous "while keeping organizations fully accountable" ([TRADE, Allouche et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13158)), and sharing structured around ATT&CK ([Ali et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10092)).

Text de-identification research supplies the cautionary results. TAB annotates 1,268 court cases with direct identifiers and quasi-identifiers so that anonymisers can be judged beyond fixed entity types ([Pilán et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1162/coli_a_00458)). Manzanares-Salor et al. argue that recall against human annotation "is not a risk metric" and measure disclosure risk with an automated re-identification attack instead ([Manzanares-Salor et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-024-01066-3)). ENISA analyses brute-force and dictionary attacks on pseudonyms ([ENISA 2019](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/pseudonymisation-techniques-and-best-practices)). Data-extraction attacks recover sensitive content from LLMs fine-tuned on CTI reports ([Batan Arachchige et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12914)). A 2026 framework for anonymising hospital CTI under NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act remains theoretical and keeps indicator utility ([Tikanmäki et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.34190/eccws.25.1.4724)). We found no work applying formal guarantees such as k-anonymity ([Sweeney 2002](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0218488502001648)) or differential privacy ([Dwork et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/11681878_14)) to sanitized incident reports.

*How ATTACKDNA differs.* It belongs to the "publish non-secrets" family, and Lincoln et al. is its closest ancestor. It changes the unit of sharing, though. Instead of sanitized alerts or indicators, it shares a free-text report reduced to behaviour (ATT&CK techniques, entry vector, attack type, CVEs), and every contributor is pseudonymised by default rather than through MISP's opt-in delegation. Its guarantee is heuristic rather than cryptographic, and it trusts its own server with the sanitized record, which PRACIS and the MPC systems do not. No reviewed system combines free-text de-identification, reduction to an ATT&CK/CVE profile and keyed pseudonyms. TRADE covers anonymous accountability, Tikanmäki et al. cover anonymisation of CTI, and Ali et al. cover ATT&CK-structured sharing, but none does all three. That is an absence in the searches behind this report, not a proof.

### 6.2 Published ATT&CK-mapping figures do not carry over from one test set to another

Automated mapping of report text to ATT&CK has moved from rules and ontologies to fine-tuned encoders and LLMs, and headline numbers swing widely with the test data. TTPDrill matched subject–verb–object threat actions to an ontology with BM25 and reported "more than 82% of precision and recall" on Symantec reports ([Husari et al. 2017](https://acsac.org/2017/program-files/s297.html)), yet LADDER's re-evaluation on five malware reports gave it F1 0.14 ([Alam et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01753)). rcATT's document-level TF-IDF classifiers over 1,490 ATT&CK-referenced reports reached technique micro precision 37.18% and recall 29.79% ([Legoy et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14322)). AttacKG aligned report graphs to templates built from 7,373 MITRE procedure examples and reported technique F1 0.789 on 16 of its own labelled reports ([Li et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.07093)), but LADDER measured it at F1 0.15, and Kumarasinghe et al. at R@3 0.13 ([Alam et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01753); [Kumarasinghe et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17068)). LADDER itself mapped extracted phrases by sentence-transformer similarity and reached P 0.65 / R 0.63 end to end on five malware reports. MITRE's TRAM ships SciBERT models for about 50 common techniques ([TRAM](https://github.com/center-for-threat-informed-defense/tram)). TTPXHunter fine-tuned SecureBERT on augmented MITRE-derived sentences and self-reported a report-level macro F1 of 97.09 on 149 reports ([Rani et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03267)). The designs most similar to ATTACKDNA's are three. Orbinato et al. trained on 12,945 MITRE procedure examples (SecureBERT F 72.5% at sentence level) and saw F fall to about 50% on six real reports ([Orbinato et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.12144)). Kumarasinghe et al.'s BM25 → SentSecBERT → MonoT5 ranker reached R@3 0.8102 and P@1 0.5783 ([Kumarasinghe et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17068)). SMET embeds CVE text and predicts techniques with logistic regression without labelled CVE–technique pairs ([Abdeen et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-37586-6_15)). A re-implementation of five methods on MITRE procedure descriptions found that F1 falls as the number of technique classes grows ([Rahman & Williams 2022a](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02601)).

The 2025 USENIX Security SoK re-implemented the field on two benchmarks built from real reports, and it is the fairest reference point. On the top-50 techniques the best systems reach "a precision of around 80% for a recall of around 66%", while for rarer techniques precision falls "way below 40% in realistic settings". Unsupervised embedding similarity to ATT&CK descriptions is "practically unusable due to their low Precision" and drops to F1 10.71 (TRAM2) and 13.44 (AnnoCTR) when all techniques are allowed. A prompted Llama-3.1-8B scores F1 49.2/30.3, rising to 54.1/35.9 with RAG and 72.5/55.3 with LoRA fine-tuning. Domain-pretrained models "do not show a consistent improvement" over generic ones, and two AnnoCTR annotators agreed on only 31% of ATT&CK concepts ([Büchel et al. 2025](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/buechel)). Independent LLM studies are harsher. Seven open models on 2,076 real DFIR Report sentences reached at best micro-F1 0.22 (0.32 with RAG), and about 40% of technique-bearing sentences carry two or more labels ([Ryan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18166)). LLMs were "inconsistent and overconfident" on 350 reports and degraded on full-length ones ([Mezzi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23175)). GPT-4 scored 0.6388 on CTIBench's extraction task, which is built from MITRE software descriptions ([Alam et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07599)). Public evaluation sets now exist: AnnoCTR with a temporal split ([Lange et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.103)), TRAM2 and CTI-HAL ([Della Penna et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05866)). Critical work recommends splitting by report, including non-technique sentences and reporting per-document scores. Attack type and initial-access vector have no standard benchmark at all. Most TTP pipelines replace CVE identifiers with placeholders before classification ([Rani et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03267); [Kumarasinghe et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17068)), whereas CISA's KEV catalog offers deterministic enrichment, including whether a CVE is "Known To Be Used in Ransomware Campaigns" ([CISA KEV](https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog)).

*How ATTACKDNA differs.* Its mapper extracts techniques from the victim's own report and then discards the text. It scores the full ATT&CK label space on a CPU, using MiniLM embeddings and a small logistic regression, a design whose closest published twin is SMET. It returns MITRE's own sentence as evidence, keeps CVEs and enriches them from KEV, and keeps the LLM optional, a choice the LLM literature supports. Its held-out test removes whole attackers and software families from training, which is stricter than the random splits used by Orbinato et al., Rahman & Williams and TTPXHunter; we found no precedent for it. It has not yet been run on AnnoCTR or TRAM2, however, so its numbers sit beside the SoK's rather than against them (section 8.2).

### 6.3 Similar-incident retrieval and response recommendation stay inside one organisation

Recalling a similar past incident and reusing its response is an old idea. Case-based reasoning (CBR) defines a retrieve–reuse–revise–retain cycle ([Aamodt & Plaza 1994](https://doi.org/10.3233/AIC-1994-7104)) that was applied to intrusion detection in 1996 ([Esmaili et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1109/CSAC.1996.569702)). According to available summaries, IRSS classifies an attack, retrieves similar past attacks and reuses adapted response plans ([Capuzzi et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1109/CTS.2006.55)), and Woskov et al. judged CBR the most suitable technique for scenario-based incident notification ([Woskov et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1109/CICYBS.2011.5949397)). ReasONets combined anomaly detection with CBR ([Squicciarini et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2557547.2557562)), and Jiang et al. retrieved emergency-response cases by description-logic similarity ([Jiang et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-44980-6_32)). CCIRS combined CBR with the IODEF standard to "retain concrete problem-solving experiences of cybersecurity incident resolution" in "a reusable memory" ([Nunes et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1142/S021819401940014X)). A 2025 framework combines cases and clusters to record, retrieve and reuse response plans; only its summary was readable ([Guerra et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.engappai.2025.111831)). Evaluations in this line are small or qualitative and share no benchmark. Work on similarity over ATT&CK profiles warns that raw technique overlap discriminates weakly. Once the ten most generic techniques are removed, pairwise Jaccard between group profiles has a **mean of 0.04 and a median of 0.02**, and "removing generic behaviors is important to build accurate behavioral signatures" ([Saha et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10645)). Weighted multi-field cosine similarity clusters APT groups well (silhouette 0.76, ARI 0.63) ([Chen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2024.3469552)). T1059 and T1105 are hubs that co-occur with many techniques ([Rahman & Williams 2022b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06495)), and AI agents emulating APT groups may erode TTP-based attribution ([Balassone et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07158)).

Industrial systems retrieve at scale, but within one customer's data and over telemetry. DeepCASE groups events by context, filtering 86.72% of events and cutting manual workload by 90.53% ([van Ede et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP46214.2022.9833671)). GraphWeaver correlates alerts into incidents across hundreds of thousands of enterprises with "a 99% accuracy rate" ([Freitas & Gharib 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01842)). NoDoze uses historical frequency to cut false alarms by an estimated 84% ([Hassan et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.14722/ndss.2019.23349)). Microsoft's Copilot Guided Response (CGR) is the strongest industrial comparator. Its similar-incident skill hashes ordered detector IDs, embeds 72 incident features, and retrieves "within the same organization" over a 180-day window; in an expert review of 1,000 incidents, 94% of its recommendations were judged relevant. Its remediation model, trained on pooled multi-organisation analyst actions with the organisation as a feature, reports 99% precision over three actions ([Freitas et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09017)). Other response knowledge is generic. D3FEND maps countermeasures to offensive techniques through a digital-artifact ontology built partly from over 500 patents ([Kaloroumakis & Smith 2021](https://d3fend.mitre.org/resources/D3FEND.pdf)), and BRON links ATT&CK, CAPEC, CWE and CVE ([Hemberg et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00533)). Of 298 NIST SP 800-53 controls, only 101 mitigate any technique and 53 techniques have no mitigating control, while 20 critical controls cover on average 72% of the techniques used by 98% of adversaries ([Rahman & Williams 2022c](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06500)). IC-SECURE recommends the next SOAR playbook module with precision@1 above 0.8 ([Kremer et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03825)). A study of 1,217 playbooks and 147 participants stresses that playbooks shared "across organizations" must preserve "effectiveness and confidentiality" ([Schlette et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP54263.2024.00060)), and CACAO offers a standard playbook format ([Empl et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10207-023-00760-5)). In IT operations, recommending mitigations from historical incidents is already mainstream ([Ahmed et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE48619.2023.00149); [RCACopilot, Chen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3627703.3629553)).

Since 2023, LLM and RAG assistants have grounded their answers in logs, CTI or documentation. Cadet et al. filter logs with a query library tagged by ATT&CK technique and answer forensic questions with 94% average recall (Claude Sonnet 4) over 17 malware scenarios ([Cadet et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18196)). Tellache et al. enrich SIEM alerts from a CTI vector store and generate mitigation strategies ([Tellache et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10677)). Hammar et al. fine-tune a lightweight model on logs from 68,000 incidents paired with response plans and prove a bound on hallucination; on 25 incidents, recovery time was 13.46 against 17.28 for OpenAI o3 ([Hammar et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05188)). IRCopilot names the failure modes as "context loss, hallucinations, privacy protection concerns, and… limited ability to provide accurate, context-specific recommendations" ([Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20945)). LocalIntel puts global threat reports into the context of one organisation's private knowledge ([Mitra et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10036)). In AutoBnB-RAG, agents that retrieve past incident narratives make better tabletop decisions ([Liu & Anwar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13118)). Field evidence favours evidence over verdicts. Across 3,090 GPT-4 queries by 45 SOC analysts over ten months, LLMs served as "on-demand cognitive aids that augment, rather than replace, SOC expertise" ([Singh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18947)). Analysts want alarms that are "Explainable", "Contextual" and "Transferable" ([Alahmadi et al. 2022](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/alahmadi)), SOCs struggle with tooling and process mismatches ([Kokulu et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3319535.3354239)), and only 14% of explainable-AI studies in security test with users ([Nadeem et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/EuroSP57164.2023.00022)).

*How ATTACKDNA differs.* It is a CBR system with three distinctive parts. Its case is an ATT&CK/CVE behaviour fingerprint. Its retrieval has two stages: a dense embedding search, then an explainable structural re-rank. Its recommendations are actions, not generated text, each labelled by origin with usage counts and effectiveness. It is evaluated across organisations, which none of the CBR or industrial systems is. It also shows on screen the evidence CGR's expert reviewers used to judge relevance (shared techniques, tactics, entry vector and CVEs). It is tiny by comparison, though: its memory holds 24 incidents, where CGR runs in production. Its structural Jaccard does not down-weight generic techniques as Saha et al. recommend. And its "response reused" metric measures coverage, not the precision@k or expert ratings that other recommenders report.

### 6.4 Next-step prediction beats popularity only modestly on ATT&CK data

The work closest to ATTACKDNA's data treats ATT&CK techniques as unordered sets. Al-Shaer et al. clustered ATT&CK group and software profiles into 98 technique associations, 78% of them with significant mutual information ([Al-Shaer et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01654)). Kuwano et al. predict that an attacker who resembles a known group will go on to use that group's other techniques ([Kuwano et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2197/ipsjjip.31.802)). MITRE's Technique Inference Engine (TIE) trains matrix-factorisation recommenders on 43,899 technique observations from 6,236 CTI reports. TIE is the only work found that reports a popularity baseline under held-out evaluation, and its gain is modest: recall@10 of **0.2613 against 0.2285**, and recall@20 of 0.4037 against 0.3419, about 14–18% relative. T1059 alone appears in a third of its reports ([Turner et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04819)). Sequence models report far larger numbers on very different data. Tiresias predicts the next of 4,495 IPS event types from 3.4 billion events with precision 0.81–0.83, against 0.52–0.62 for a Markov chain ([Shen et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3243734.3243811)). HMMs ([Holgado et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/TDSC.2017.2751478)), Bayesian attack graphs ([Poolsappasit et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1109/TDSC.2011.34)), automata learned from alerts ([Nadeem et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/TDSC.2021.3117348)) and bidirectional deep models over logs ([Li et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/TDSC.2022.3143551)) all predict over alerts or network models. An LSTM–Markov hybrid claims 86% next-step accuracy on 4,849 chains drawn from only 33 independent campaigns ([Raj et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.5220/0015075400004103)). A knowledge-graph embedding trained on ChatGPT-generated multi-step templates reports Hits@1 above Hits@10, which cannot be right ([Xiang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1051/sands/2024019)). On AttackSeqBench the best LLM recovers a hidden tactic with 0.5903 accuracy zero-shot, against 0.51 for humans ([Ma et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03170)). The near-perfect F1 in KillChainGraph measures the assignment of techniques to kill-chain phases, not forecasting ([KillChainGraph, Singh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18230)). Kill-chain models supply the stage vocabulary ([Hutchins et al. 2011](https://www.lockheedmartin.com/content/dam/lockheed-martin/rms/documents/cyber/LM-White-Paper-Intel-Driven-Defense.pdf); [Unified Kill Chain](https://www.unifiedkillchain.com/)). The standard survey flags evaluation as the weak point of the whole area ([Husák et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/COMST.2018.2871866)). MITRE created Attack Flow because "tracking adversary behaviors one action at a time makes it hard to build effective defenses against multi-phased attacks" ([Attack Flow](https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/attack-flow/)), a sign that ordered ATT&CK data is scarce.

*How ATTACKDNA differs.* The prototype shows only a record of what similar past incidents did next. Its unmerged predictive model gains 3.4 points over popularity overall and 7.7 points on threat groups and campaigns (section 8.4), the same order as TIE's gain on similar data. Keeping the model out until it clearly beats the baseline, and labelling the record "not a prediction", is more conservative than the "next move" framing of MITRE's own blog post about TIE ([CTID blog](https://ctid.mitre.org/blog/2024/09/09/know-your-adversarys-next-move-with-tie/)).

## 7. Methodology and System Architecture

### 7.1 One pipeline, three entry points and a privacy gate

Everything runs through one function, `analyze()`, which "runs the whole chain and returns the result without writing anything". `contribute()` is "the security team's consent step": it stores an already analysed incident from its sanitized text and DNA alone. `ingest()` combines the two for loading corpora. "The privacy check is a gate, not a label. If the self-check still finds an identifier, the text is not sent to the optional LLM and it cannot be contributed to shared memory" ([analyze.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py)). In code, `analyze()` calls `sanitize()` and `verify_clean()`, disables the LLM if anything leaks, and then runs DNA extraction, similar-incident search, mitigation recall and an optional tabletop simulation. The README draws five stages: ① privacy layer, ② Attack DNA extraction, ③ similar incidents with explainable re-ranking, ④ mitigation memory, and ⑤ safe simulation ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)).

### 7.2 Stage 1: privacy by rules, not by a model

Identifiers with a recognisable shape are caught by regular expressions. These include addresses, mailboxes, host names, accounts, ticket numbers, secrets, IBANs, card numbers, national IDs, phone numbers and user paths ([sanitizer.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/sanitizer.py)). Identifiers without a shape (people, organisations, places) are found by reading capitalisation against three vocabularies: ordinary English words; public security terms ("every technique, malware, group, vendor and product name MITRE and CISA publish"); and given names. "A capitalised token that is in none of them is a name" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The layer fails closed: "An unfamiliar product name is lost; an unfamiliar person is not leaked." It withholds whole any text in a script it cannot read, including Arabic, which "has no capital letter to mark a name". It maps the same value to the same token, so analysis is lossless, and it protects CVE IDs, ATT&CK IDs, file hashes and registry keys. Its `verify_clean()` check is explicitly "a self-check, not independent verification — the independent measurement is the hand-marked audit". It does not claim "that free text is anonymous" (same source).

### 7.3 Stage 2: Attack DNA from rules, MITRE sentences and an optional LLM

The DNA holds the attack type; the initial vector; techniques and tactics, "each with the evidence behind it"; CVEs with their KEV status; impacts; a severity "derived from impact, KEV status and chain length"; and a signature in kill-chain order. Attack type and entry vector are kept apart: "phishing that ends in ransomware is a **ransomware** incident that *arrived by* phishing" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The taxonomy has ten attack types (ransomware, phishing, business email compromise, data breach, web exploitation, credential attack, insider threat, supply chain, denial of service and malware infection) and six impact classes ([dna_extractor.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)). A deterministic rule layer "always runs and always produces a complete DNA". An optional LLM adds a summary, extra techniques and extra impacts and is "merged conservatively": "the rules win on ties", and techniques the LLM proposes are re-run through the mapper (same source). The default models are Anthropic `claude-opus-5` and Gemini `gemini-2.5-flash`, chosen by the key's prefix. Without a key the system runs in rule-based mode; with one, the interface uses the LLM automatically ([frontend/app.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/frontend/app.py)).

Techniques come by three routes: "an id the report states, a phrase from a curated table, or resemblance to the ~17,600 sentences MITRE has labelled". The learned route blends a small logistic regression over MiniLM sentence embeddings ([Wang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10957); [Reimers & Gurevych 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)) with nearest-example votes, weighted 70/30. It "abstains unless its neighbours agree" and attaches the MITRE sentence, not the report's words, as evidence. Negated statements are not counted: "'no data was exfiltrated' is not exfiltration" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md); [example_mapper.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/example_mapper.py)). Its thresholds were "chosen on development data only" ([example_mapper.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/example_mapper.py)). On the same held-out MITRE sentences, recall rose from 20.8% with rules alone to 44.0% with a TF-IDF learner ([Salton & Buckley 1988](https://doi.org/10.1016/0306-4573(88)90021-0)), then to 64.9% with MiniLM and 81.6% with the classifier (commits `ea7c40c`, `412a3b8` and `f1c70e0`). Indicators are split into two groups. Shareable ones (file hashes, CVEs, ransomware extensions, protocols, ports, registry keys) are kept. Victim-linked ones (addresses, domains, mailboxes, hosts, accounts) are removed, and only their counts are kept. Threat-actor resemblance compares the technique set with the documented techniques of 172 groups and 825 malware families and tools, weighted by technique specificity, and labels matches weak, moderate or notable, "never 'confirmed'". Every extraction is validated against a schema that forbids redaction tokens or email addresses in the text that gets embedded ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)).

### 7.4 Stage 3: vector memory and an explainable re-rank

"Only DNA-derived text is embedded — never the report — so the vector store is structurally incapable of holding victim identifiers." The default backend is ChromaDB's bundled ONNX version of `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`; sentence-transformers and an offline TF-IDF fallback are the alternatives ([vector_memory.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/vector_memory.py)). Retrieval over-fetches max(3 × top_k, 10) candidates and re-ranks them as **0.45 semantic + 0.55 structural**. Within the structural score, technique Jaccard weighs 0.45, tactic Jaccard 0.20, attack type 0.18, initial vector 0.10 and CVEs 0.07. Every match carries its `match_reasons` and sub-scores, and sector is never used ([similarity.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/similarity.py)). The README gives an empirical reason for the structural term: on the demo corpus, "five ransomware incidents all score 0.92–0.97 semantically, and only structural overlap separates them" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)).

### 7.5 Stage 4: mitigation memory with labelled origins

Each recommendation carries one of three origins. `recalled` is what a past incident's team actually did, cited back to that incident; `framework` is an official ATT&CK mitigation (M####); and `suggested` is "a concrete baseline control filling a gap neither covers" ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). A recalled action's score sums similarity × recorded effectiveness over the incidents that used it, with 0.6 standing in when no effectiveness was recorded, and multiplies the total by 1 + 0.15 × (times used − 1). At most twelve actions are kept, ordered by response phase, and the interface shows the three strongest in each phase ([mitigation_memory.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/mitigation_memory.py); [frontend/app.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/frontend/app.py)). A fifth stage builds a defensive tabletop exercise that contains no payloads, commands or exploit code, which tests assert; it is computed but no longer shown in the interface ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)).

### 7.6 Contribution needs the team's consent and stores keyed pseudonyms

`contribute()` re-runs `verify_clean()` and refuses with `PrivacyGateError` if anything remains. It sanitizes the free-text action and notes of each mitigation and skips duplicates. It stores `raw_text=None` and `occurred_at=None`, sets the share date to the first day of the current month, and derives the title from the DNA alone ("Ransomware via phishing"). It returns the list of what was withheld: "original report", "organisation name", "removed identifiers" and "date of the incident" ([analyze.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py)). The organisation is stored as "HMAC-SHA256 of the normalised organisation name under a server-side key" and compared in constant time. "The pseudonym never leaves the server", and responses carry only the result of the comparison, `from_your_organisation` ([contributors.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/contributors.py)).

### 7.7 Ask the memory gives three answers from the same pipeline

The description of a live attack goes through the same privacy layer, DNA extraction, similar-incident search and mitigation memory. `guidance.py` then forms three answers as "pure functions over plain dicts: no database, no model". `what_worked` lists recalled actions first, and baseline actions only when memory has none, up to five. `same_entry` lists up to three similar incidents with the identical initial vector, each with its kill chain, up to six techniques and its three most effective actions. `next_steps` finds the furthest tactic reached so far and then counts the top four later tactics across similar incidents, with up to three example techniques each, explicitly "a record of what happened in similar past incidents, not a prediction" ([guidance.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/guidance.py)). If the privacy check fails, the page stops ([frontend/app.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/frontend/app.py)). An older retrieval-augmented question-answering module, `rag_qa.py`, which follows the RAG pattern ([Lewis et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)) with verified citations, remains available at `POST /ask` but is not used by the interface ([rag_qa.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/rag_qa.py); [routes/search.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/routes/search.py)).

The ten public breaches are Norsk Hydro, Colonial Pipeline, Change Healthcare, MOVEit, NotPetya, Equifax, Target, SolarWinds, Kaseya and Uber; the 14 synthetic incidents invent every organisation, person and identifier ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The KEV snapshot lags the live catalog, which listed 1,781 entries on 14 November 2026 ([CISA KEV](https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog)). The stack is a FastAPI backend, SQLAlchemy with SQLite, ChromaDB and a Streamlit frontend, which calls the pipeline in-process by default ([docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md); [frontend/app.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/frontend/app.py)). The 369 tests include fully offline operation with every outbound socket blocked, determinism, and a 5-second latency budget per prepared scenario ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md); [tests/test_demo_readiness.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/tests/test_demo_readiness.py)).

### 7.9 Evaluation method

The evaluation design is the project's strongest methodological feature. An early evaluation "reported 100% on eight cases — the same eight the rules had been adjusted against". The team then froze the code and ran the held-out sets once, afterwards (commit `8fc50d5`). The privacy audit checks hand-marked identifiers "with a plain substring test on the sanitized output, so it does not depend on what the privacy layer believes it detected" ([privacy_audit.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/seed/privacy_audit.json)). It replaced an earlier evaluation that asked the sanitizer's own detector, so "the number could only ever confirm itself" (commit `f88bc8a`). Attack type is scored on lead summaries of real incidents "taken verbatim from English Wikipedia", labelled before the system was run ([evaluation_heldout.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/seed/evaluation_heldout.json)). Technique mapping holds out every sentence about one fifth of the groups and software families (214 sources, 3,945 sentences), masks the sentence's subject and scores at parent-technique level ([evaluate_mapper.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/scripts/evaluate_mapper.py)). The cross-organisation experiment pairs 20 attack patterns, each reported once by contributor A and later "in different words" by B, all written after the rules were frozen. A's 20 incidents sit among the 24 corpus incidents, which act as distractors, in a throwaway database ([crossorg_pairs.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/seed/crossorg_pairs.json); [evaluate_crossorg.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/scripts/evaluate_crossorg.py)). Intervals are 95% Wilson intervals.

### 8.1 Privacy: the 98.6% figure was recomputed after fixes; the first run gave about 97%

The 112 texts combine 40 held-out privacy sentences (53 identifiers), 32 Wikipedia summaries (55 identifiers) and the 40 cross-organisation reports (171 identifiers) ([results.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/scripts/results.py)). All four surviving identifiers and both lost terms are in the cross-organisation reports. The survivors are a phone number written with spaces ("+971 4 555 0192"), two one-word company names ("Mirage" and "Wafa") and a storage-bucket name ("wafa-claims-backup"). The self-check missed three of them, and the README reports them "not fixed after the fact" ([crossorg_results.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/evaluation/crossorg_results.json); [README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The headline figure nevertheless mixes first-run and post-fix results. When the held-out privacy sentences and Wikipedia summaries were first run on frozen code, they found four leaks: a machine name before a full stop, "ICICI" read as a roman numeral, and "23andMe". The first-run scores were 51/53 and 53/55, and "the fixes here come after that run and do not change those figures" (commit `ea7c40c`); an earlier README reported **96%** for exactly that reason. Today those 108 identifiers score 108/108, on code that includes the fixes. Only the cross-organisation component (167/171, 97.7%) is a strict first-run figure. Combining the first-run counts gives 271/279, about **97.1%** (our arithmetic). The README's claim that "every figure comes from data the system was not tuned on" is therefore weaker for privacy than for the other rows.

The most informative privacy result is not the percentage but the run with the privacy layer switched off. Retrieval quality was identical (precision@1 0.55, recall@5 0.95), while stored records exposing A fell from **20 of 20 to 1 of 20** ([crossorg_results.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/evaluation/crossorg_results.json)). On this set, privacy cost nothing in utility. The one remaining record (pair p18A) explains why the two exposure figures differ. B's screen shows only DNA-derived fields, so no screen named A; but shared records also store the sanitized text, and that is where the two missed "Wafa" identifiers sit.

### 8.2 Technique mapping is strong on single sentences and weaker on whole reports

The 81.6% recall comes from 3,218 of 3,945 held-out sentences. The mapper made 5,356 suggestions, about 1.36 per sentence, with 60.1% precision, which the project calls "a lower bound: each sentence has a single label" ([mapper_results.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/evaluation/mapper_results.json); [README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The lower-bound claim is plausible, because about 40% of technique-bearing sentences in real reports carry two or more techniques ([Ryan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18166)), but it stays a hypothesis until a sample of false positives is re-annotated. The figure is also generous in two ways: it scores parent techniques, with sub-techniques folded in, and each test sentence describes exactly one technique. On whole reports, precision rises to 83.6% while recall falls to **49.2%**. The README quotes this figure on raw text; on the sanitized text the system actually stores, it is 81.1% precision and 48.4% recall ([crossorg_results.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/evaluation/crossorg_results.json)). The gap is small, which is itself evidence that redaction preserves behaviour.

These numbers can be set beside published results but not ranked against them, because the data, units and label spaces differ.

Two readings follow. First, ATTACKDNA's sentence-level F1 of 69.2% under a held-out-attacker split is close to Orbinato's 72.5% under random cross-validation, on a stricter split, and its whole-report profile (high precision, recall near 50%) resembles Orbinato's document result and the SoK's precision-heavy best systems. Second, AttacKG's fall from 0.79 on its own reports to 0.15 on others' is a warning that a figure measured on the team's own synthetic reports should not be assumed to hold on practitioners' writing. The SoK's finding that pure embedding similarity collapses when every technique is allowed suggests why ATTACKDNA is more precise: it uses procedure sentences, adds a supervised vote and abstains when unsure. That remains a hypothesis, since no ablation against the SoK setting has been run. Attack type (24/28 on real summaries) and initial vector have no public benchmark to compare against, and the 92.5% and 100% on the cross-organisation reports measure agreement with the team's own labels.

### 8.3 Across organisations: A's incident came first for 11 of 20 attacks and in the top five for 19

A's incident ranked first for 11 queries, second for 5 (p12, p13, p14, p16 and p19), third for 2, fourth for 1 and sixth for 1. The sixth-place case, p06, is an MFA-fatigue pattern for which the top result was the public Uber incident ([crossorg_results.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/evaluation/crossorg_results.json)). The README's phrase "second in 7 of the other 9" does not match this file; "second or third in 7 of the other 9" would. With no distractors, precision@1 rises to 14/20 and recall@5 to 20/20, so the 24 corpus incidents really do compete. The figure has moved with the code: 14/20 before the technique classifier and the sector removal (commit `55954f7`), 13/20 just before the sector removal, and 11/20 now. The README states the cost of dropping sector as 65% → 55%, with top-five and response reuse unchanged ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). A query counts as "response reused" when at least one action A recorded is recommended to B as `recalled` with A's incident cited ([evaluate_crossorg.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/scripts/evaluate_crossorg.py)). That shows the provenance chain works (no recommendation was unattributed), but not that the recommended actions were the right ones. When A re-read its own report, it saw "yours" in 19 of 20 cases, and no foreign incident was ever marked as B's own.

The top-five result fits the shortlist presentation CGR also uses, and a top-1 rate near 55% is consistent with Saha et al.'s finding that technique profiles are only moderately distinctive. The interval (34–74%) is too wide to tell the current configuration apart from the 65% measured before the sector was removed.

### 8.4 Next-step prediction: six profiles out of 177

The predictive module lives on branch `claude/eager-cray-db8wv0` (commit `6411856`, pull request #16). It finds the furthest kill-chain stage of a partial profile and takes the 50 most similar of about 930 ATT&CK group, software and campaign profiles, plus incidents in memory. It then scores later-stage techniques by their weighted share, leaving out ten near-universal techniques (T1027, T1036, T1057, T1059, T1070, T1071, T1082, T1083, T1105 and T1140). Shown the first half of 177 held-out profiles, at least one of its three predictions was a later step in **100 cases, against 94** for always guessing the most common later steps (56.5% vs 53.1%). On the 39 threat groups and campaigns the counts were **26 against 23** (66.7% vs 59.0%), and on the 138 malware and tool profiles 74 against 71 ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md); `next_step_results.json` on the branch). No intervals were published. Our Wilson intervals (49–64% and 51–79%) contain the baseline rates, though a paired test would be the proper check. As the team notes, "later" means a later kill-chain stage of a profile that summarises many intrusions, not a time-ordered step within one incident. The team judged that "the lead is small and the sample modest" and kept the module out of the prototype ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)), a judgement the literature supports, since TIE's gain over popularity on comparable data is of the same order.

### 8.5 What has not been measured yet

Several gaps limit what the results can claim. There is **no LLM-mode evaluation**. Every published figure is rule-based, yet the interface switches the LLM on automatically whenever a key is present, so the configuration most users with a key would see is unevaluated. The 40 cross-organisation reports are synthetic, written and labelled by the project team, with no inter-annotator agreement, and no real SOC report has been tested ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The whole-report thresholds were tuned on "76 team-labelled development reports" that are not listed anywhere. The development sets total 36 cases, and 36 + 40 = 76, so the cross-organisation reports may have helped set `REPORT_FLOOR` and `MAX_LEARNED_PER_REPORT`; the repository cannot settle this, and if they did, the 84%/49% figure is not independent ([example_mapper.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/example_mapper.py)). The cross-organisation results file records code revision `4f4d8d4` but was generated three minutes before the commit that added it, so it reflects uncommitted code. No held-out initial-vector, impact or severity result on the Wikipedia set is committed. The British Library case study is qualitative and has no committed artefact. It covers an 18-page review of a 2024 Rhysida ransomware attack, which the system read as ransomware via compromised credentials with 14 techniques, and it first "exposed three faults" that were fixed after the report had been seen: "Rhysida", "Terminal Server" and "Multi-Factor Authentication" were redacted as if they were organisation names, and both the sector and the vector were wrong (commit `2a5978d`). Latency has no hardware specification. Finally, older documents still carry superseded figures that should not be quoted:

## 9. Innovation and Added Value

ATTACKDNA's innovation is a combination, not a single component, and the literature review shows exactly which combination. Each part has precedents. Deterministic sanitisation for contributor privacy goes back to Lincoln et al. (2004); keyed pseudonymisation to prefix-preserving IP anonymisation and ENISA's guidance; ATT&CK-structured sharing to Ali et al. (2021); retrieving similar past incidents and reusing their responses to IRSS, CCIRS and CGR; and co-occurrence views of what attackers do next to TIE and Kuwano et al. What we did not find in any reviewed system is all of these together: free-text de-identification of a victim's own report; reduction of that report to an ATT&CK/CVE behaviour profile; keyed pseudonyms for every contributor by default; and cross-organisation recall of responses labelled by origin, usage and effectiveness, evaluated with an explicit test of whether the other party learned who the victim was.

The central insight is that the most useful layer of threat intelligence is also the least identifying. The Pyramid of Pain puts TTPs at the top because they are hardest for an attacker to change ([Bianco 2013](http://detect-respond.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-pyramid-of-pain.html)); the IP addresses, domains, hosts and accounts at the bottom are exactly what identifies the victim. Sharing only the top layer therefore improves utility and privacy with one design decision. The project measured this trade-off instead of asserting it. With the privacy layer on, stored records naming A fell from 20/20 to 1/20 at no retrieval cost, and removing the sector, a quasi-identifier, cost 2 of 20 first-place matches and nothing in the top five ([crossorg_results.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/evaluation/crossorg_results.json); [README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The privacy literature asks for this kind of evidence, and platform designs rarely report it.

The second contribution is provenance. Each recommended action says whether it was recalled from past incidents (with how many used it and how well it worked), taken from the ATT&CK mitigation catalogue, or supplied as a baseline control. No reviewed recommender exposes this. CGR learns from analyst actions but hides where a recommendation came from, and its expert reviewers judged relevance by shared attack patterns that the product does not display; ATTACKDNA displays those same fields ([Freitas et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09017)). NIST SP 800-61r3's call to record "what actions were taken, and how effective those actions were" gives this design a normative anchor ([NIST SP 800-61r3](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-61r3.pdf)). Field evidence that analysts use LLMs as cognitive aids rather than oracles supports an interface that leads with evidence ([Singh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18947)). And because every recommendation points to a stored case or a named source, there is no generated text for an LLM to hallucinate, the failure mode IRCopilot and others identify in generated advice ([Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20945)).

The third contribution is evaluation discipline that is unusual for a student prototype and uncommon in CBR work on incident response. It includes a code freeze before held-out runs, first-run reporting, an independent substring audit in place of a self-check, Wilson intervals, a held-out-attacker split for which we found no precedent, a privacy-off ablation, and a predictive feature withheld because it did not clearly beat a popularity baseline. The "record, not prediction" wording is more careful than some published framing of similar recommenders.

There is practical value too. The prototype runs fully offline on one machine with no API key, which suits organisations that cannot send incident data to cloud services. It uses only public knowledge (ATT&CK and KEV) and trains on nothing private. Its behaviour-level record matches the "operational CTI" category in SAMA's principles, which gives it a regional regulatory anchor ([SAMA Rulebook](https://rulebook.sama.gov.sa/en/entiresection/3087)). And a design that never reveals *who* was attacked depends less on legal shields that, as the history of the CISA 2015 Act shows, can lapse. The poster's qualitative comparison (its Table 2) marks typical IoC sharing and plain similarity search as lacking match explanations, recall of what worked and records of what attackers did next ([poster](./ATTACKDNA_SAIF_Poster_SAIF130191.pdf)). That holds against those two generic baselines, but the literature shows partial overlaps: MISP's pseudo-anonymous publishing, response reuse in CBR systems and CGR, and TIE's co-occurrence inference. The defensible claim is therefore the combination and its cross-organisation evaluation, not each capability on its own.

## 10. Limitations

### 10.1 What is built and what is still only a description

The core loop works end to end on one machine. A report passes the privacy gate, becomes an Attack DNA, is matched against memory with reasons, and returns actions with their sources. Contribution is consent-gated and stored under an HMAC pseudonym, there is a dashboard, and Ask the memory gives its three answers ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). The multi-organisation platform is not built. "The organisation this installation belongs to is set once in `backend/.env` (`ATTACKDNA_ORGANISATION`), as a login would set it in a deployed platform". There are no accounts, roles or access control, and cross-organisation use is simulated by contributing as A and querying as B in one database ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md); [config.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/config.py); [evaluate_crossorg.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/scripts/evaluate_crossorg.py)). The table compares the team's description of the platform with what the code does.

| Team's description | What the code does | Status |
|---|---|---|

| An attacked organisation adds its report, and the attacker's method is kept in shared memory | The Add to memory switch calls `contribute()`: privacy gate re-run; sanitized text, DNA and sanitized actions stored; HMAC pseudonym; share month only; no raw report | **Implemented** (single machine) |

| During an attack, the team describes what the attacker did and how they got in, and memory recalls similar behaviour | Ask the memory returns similar incidents and `same_entry` (same vector, with their chains and techniques) | **Implemented** |
| …the attacker's likely next methods | `next_steps` counts later tactics in similar past incidents, "a record, not a prediction"; the predictive model exists only in unmerged PR #16 | **Partly**: record only |
| …and how they were stopped | `what_worked` shows recalled actions with effectiveness; but actions entered in the UI are stored as category "contain" with no effectiveness, which can be recorded only through `POST /incidents/{id}/mitigations` or seed data | **Implemented; effectiveness capture incomplete** |
| After the attack, analysing a report returns similar incidents and preventive measures | Four-section Analyze page; official ATT&CK mitigations are computed but not shown | **Implemented** |

Because effectiveness cannot be entered in the interface, the effectiveness figures on screen, such as the poster's "avg. effectiveness 92%", come from seeded or API-recorded data, and actions contributed through the interface fall back to the default weight of 0.6. Several computed features are hidden from the interface "for the next version": threat-actor resemblance, the indicator profile, official ATT&CK mitigations, the tabletop exercise, the downloadable report and the LLM settings ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). There is no STIX, TAXII or MISP export; "STIX" appears only in the script that reads MITRE's bundle ([scripts/process_cti.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/scripts/process_cti.py)). The interface and the privacy layer handle English only, and Arabic text in a report is withheld whole. That matters for the Saudi and GCC context the project targets.

### 10.2 Evaluation limitations

The evaluation is careful but small. With 20 cross-organisation queries, precision@1 has an interval of 34–74%, and with 28 real summaries, attack type has 69–94%. The 40 reports are synthetic and team-labelled. There is no independent annotation, no SOC report, no user study, no LLM-mode result and no committed artefact for the British Library case. The technique results have not been measured on the public benchmarks that would make them comparable (AnnoCTR, TRAM2 and the DFIR Report set), nor with per-document averaged F1, the SoK's convention. Response reuse measures coverage; neither the precision@k of recommended actions against what B actually did nor expert ratings, as IC-SECURE and CGR report, has been measured. The 98.6% privacy figure mixes first-run and post-fix results (section 8.1), and the independence of the whole-report thresholds cannot be confirmed (section 8.5).

### 10.3 Privacy and security limitations

Identifier recall is not a measure of disclosure risk ([Manzanares-Salor et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-024-01066-3)). No re-identification attack has been run against stored Attack DNA. Nothing checks that a combination of attack type, vector, technique set and CVEs is shared by several contributors before it becomes visible, so a rare CVE or a distinctive toolset can act as a quasi-identifier; as the project itself notes, "details such as timing and scale can still point to an organization" ([crossorg_results.json](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/data/evaluation/crossorg_results.json)). Shared records store sanitized text as well as DNA, so any sanitizer miss sits in shared storage. The README's claim that "no free text from the report can enter" the DNA holds in rule-based mode; with the LLM on, the embedded text ends with an LLM-written summary of the sanitized report ([dna_extractor.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/dna_extractor.py)). And because pseudonyms never leave the server, members get no reputation signal about a contributor's reliability, which the trust literature treats as important ([Wagner et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9634507)).

Reading the API code for this report surfaced four issues. They matter once the service is exposed beyond a local demo, a step the README and the CORS comment in `main.py` already anticipate ([main.py](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/main.py)).

**The pseudonym comparison can be used as an oracle.** `/incidents/analyze` computes the viewer's pseudonym from an `organisation` string supplied in the request body ([routes/incidents.py L41–50](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/routes/incidents.py)), and each match reports `from_your_organisation` ([similarity.py L146](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/services/similarity.py)). Without authentication, any client can submit guessed organisation names and learn which recalled incidents a named organisation contributed. This online check sidesteps the protection that the server-side key gives against offline dictionary attacks.

**Other contributors' sanitized text is readable.** `GET /incidents/` and `GET /incidents/{id}` return every stored record's `sanitized_text`, including other contributors' ([routes/incidents.py L86–120](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/routes/incidents.py); [db/database.py L112–145](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/db/database.py)). The residual identifiers that never reached B's screen can therefore be read through the API.

**Anyone can delete or re-rate contributions, and corpus loading bypasses the gate.** `DELETE /incidents/{id}` and `POST /incidents/{id}/mitigations` are unauthenticated, so any client can delete or re-rate another organisation's contribution. `ingest()` (`POST /incidents/`) stores records without the privacy gate that `contribute()` enforces and can keep the raw report when `store_raw` is set ([analyze.py L107–136](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/backend/app/pipelines/analyze.py)); that is appropriate for loading corpora, but it is exposed on the same API.

Restricting CORS to localhost limits browsers, not other HTTP clients. None of these issues affects the single-machine Streamlit flow, where the organisation is fixed in configuration. All of them belong in the threat model of the planned deployment, which does not exist yet.

### 10.4 Technical limitations

The structural re-rank uses plain Jaccard over techniques, so generic techniques such as T1059 or T1105 count as much as rare ones, against Saha et al.'s advice. The attribution module weights by specificity and the unmerged predictor excludes universal techniques, but the similarity score does neither. The Attack DNA stores an unordered technique set, although ordered representations such as Attack Flow exist. The memory holds 24 incidents, so nothing is known about retrieval quality or latency at the scale of hundreds of organisations. The incentive problem described in the free-riding literature is not addressed either: privacy lowers the cost of contributing but gives no reason to contribute.

## 11. Future Work

The README sets the order of next steps. They are larger, independently labelled evaluation sets that include practitioner reports; a pilot with a SOC on its own sanitized reports; next-step prediction once it clearly beats the frequency baseline; export of Attack DNA in STIX 2.1; Arabic-language reports; and a multi-organisation deployment with accounts and access control ([README.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/README.md)). PHASE_COVERAGE adds federated memory, Sigma detection-rule output and an automatic feedback loop on mitigation effectiveness ([docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md](https://github.com/norahs21/attackdna/blob/81b0378/docs/PHASE_COVERAGE.md)). The related work suggests how to make each step convincing.

For evaluation, the most defensible next move is to run the mapper unchanged on the AnnoCTR test split and on the TRAM2 test split restricted to its 50 labels. The results should be reported as per-document averaged precision, recall and F1, against the SoK's published figures (70.55/62.75 for labelled models and 50.6/35.5 for embedding similarity alone) ([Büchel et al. 2025](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/buechel)). Ryan et al.'s DFIR Report sentences, which include sentences with no technique, are a further candidate ([Ryan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18166)). An LLM-mode run should be published beside the rule-based one. Recommendation quality should be measured as the precision@k of recalled actions against what B actually did, plus practitioner ratings. For privacy, a re-identification attack in the style of Manzanares-Salor et al. would turn sanitizer recall into a risk measure: train a classifier to guess the contributor from stored DNA and report its accuracy. A k-anonymity threshold on DNA combinations, and a hybrid of the deterministic rules with an NER pass such as Presidio, are natural extensions. For retrieval, IDF-weighting of shared techniques, with generic ones marked as weak evidence in the explanation, follows directly from Saha et al. An ordered DNA modelled on Attack Flow would allow sequence-aware matching and give next-step prediction a real order to learn from.

For the platform, the deployment step needs authentication and per-organisation access control, with the server deriving the organisation from the login, which closes the pseudonym oracle. It also needs `sanitized_text` removed from cross-organisation API responses, ownership checks on deleting and rating contributions, a separate administrative path for loading corpora, effectiveness capture in the interface, and a written threat model that covers who holds the pseudonym key. Interoperability can follow the standards gap identified in section 3. An STIX 2.1 Incident extension could link Attack Pattern objects (ATT&CK IDs) and Vulnerability objects (CVEs), with `created_by_ref` omitted or pointing to a pseudonymous identity. Recalled responses could be exported as CACAO playbooks, with D3FEND countermeasures shown beside ATT&CK mitigations. Arabic support needs a way to detect names that does not rely on capitalisation. A reputation signal for pseudonymous contributors, as in TrustFed-CTI ([Mrabet 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/fi17110512)), could address the incentive gap.

The long-term vision is a global cybersecurity platform, and the path there is incremental. A national pilot aligned with the NCA's sharing platform and SAMA's operational-CTI category would test the model where regulation already asks for behaviour-level intelligence. A GCC stage could interoperate with the regional platform being built with SITE. A global stage would publish Attack DNA over STIX/TAXII, so that existing MISP and OpenCTI communities could use it alongside their indicators rather than in place of them. At that scale, a federated memory, in which organisations or regional hubs hold their own records and answer similarity queries without pooling them, would reduce the trust placed in a single server, and the cryptographic strands of the literature (private aggregation, MPC) would become relevant to the matching step. The global version would keep the project's strongest property: because it never needs to know who was attacked, it depends less on the legal protections and trust relationships that have limited every indicator-sharing programme so far.

## 12. Conclusion

ATTACKDNA's most important result is a measured trade-off rather than a headline accuracy. Removing the victim from shared incident data, and sharing behaviour instead of indicators, cost nothing in retrieval on the project's test set, while it cut stored exposure of the contributor from every record to one. That turns privacy-preserving sharing from a cost to be minimised into a by-product of choosing the right unit of exchange, the top of the Pyramid of Pain. It is the argument the project should lead with when it meets SOCs and regulators.

What the project has not yet shown is that the result holds up in real conditions: practitioners' own reports, independent labels, an LLM in the loop, an adversary querying the API, and a memory serving many organisations. The prototype's evaluation discipline (freezing the code before testing, reporting first runs, holding back a predictor that barely beats its baseline) is the right foundation for that stage. Applying the same discipline to public benchmarks, a re-identification attack and an authenticated multi-organisation deployment would turn a convincing prototype into evidence that a national or global platform can be built on.
